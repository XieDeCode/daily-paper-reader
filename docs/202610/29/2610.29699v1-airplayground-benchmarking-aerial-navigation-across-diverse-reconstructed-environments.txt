## ABSTRACT 

Large-scale aerial resources provide a foundation for learning navigation across diverse environments. However, existing resources remain limited in providing (1) densely sampled flight demonstrations for learning different policy formula-tions and (2) broad environment coverage for standardized closed-loop evaluation. To address these limitations, we introduce AirPlayground, a unified benchmark for training and evaluating aerial navigation policies across diverse environments. We first construct 94 photorealistic 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) environments spanning indoor, urban outdoor, and vegetation settings, with aligned collision geometry. Building on these environments, we develop a scene-adaptive pipeline for automated expert flight collection, yielding 94K demonstrations and 46.5M synchronized observation–state–action samples at 50 Hz. We further provide an extensible API that supports training different policy formulations on this shared corpus and evaluating them under unified protocols in the corresponding envi-ronments. We benchmark seven baselines spanning four policy paradigms across three navigation settings in seen and unseen environments, and further examine scaling behavior with ACT. Scaling ACT’s training corpus from 5 to 70 scenes raises median success rates on seen and unseen environments from 30 .6% and 

16 .8% to 41 .2% and 29 .4% , respectively. Through this shared training and evalu-ation framework, AirPlayground supports analysis of how offline prediction errors relate to closed-loop outcomes. Our analyses highlight the roles of prediction er-rors, execution protocols, and scene interactions in closed-loop performance. 1arXiv:2610.29699v1 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

Learning-based aerial navigation depends on large-scale, diverse experience to generalize across environments (Zheng et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Acquiring experience through online interaction can be costly and difficult to scale. Offline demonstrations support scaling policy learning without environment interaction during training (O’Neill et al., 2024). Yet policies trained on demonstrations learn from expert states, whereas deployment exposes them to states shaped by their actions (Seo et al., 2024). Small errors can compound through feedback, so accurate imitation does not guarantee reliable navigation. Assessing reliability therefore requires systematic closed-loop evaluation across diverse environments, where policies must respond to the consequences of their actions. To meet these learning and evaluation needs, aerial navigation research has produced both flight datasets and interactive environments. Large-scale datasets such as CityNav and OpenFly provide aerial trajectories for navigation and vision-language learning (Lee et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026), substantially increasing the scale and diversity of reusable flight experience. In parallel, 3DGS-based embodied platforms make reconstructed real-world scenes available as photorealistic, geometrically grounded environments for interactive navigation (Liu et al., 2026b; Miao et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026). Recent benchmarks further combine reconstructed environments, collision-aware interaction, and flight trajectories for policy learning and evaluation (Guo et al., 2026). Despite this progress, two limitations remain in the resources for aerial policy learning and eval-uation. (i) Limited dense flight experience. Densely sampled observation–action demonstrations remain limited across diverse real-world environments. Dense supervision captures continuous flight control for learning different action representations, while coverage across scenes exposes policies to varied visual conditions and spatial structures. (ii) Limited standardized evaluation across diverse environments. Standardized closed-loop evaluation remains limited in its coverage of en-vironments with diverse appearances and spatial structures. Indoor spaces, urban outdoor areas, and vegetation present different visibility and obstacle configurations, making evaluation across these settings important for assessing how consistently policies perform beyond a narrow set of scenes. To address these limitations, we introduce AirPlayground, a unified benchmark for aerial navigation that pairs dense flight experience with photorealistic 3DGS reconstructions and executable geometry. AirPlayground comprises 94 reconstructed scenes spanning indoor, urban outdoor, and vegetation environments, with nearly 100K flight demonstrations and 46.5M RGB frames under 50 Hz observation–action supervision. To scale collection, we develop a scene-adaptive pipeline, reusable across reconstructed scenes, that models navigable space, samples navigation tasks, and generates expert demonstrations. We further introduce an extensible cross-policy API that adapts the shared corpus to each policy’s native representation and maps its outputs to a common control interface under unified evaluation protocols. By linking recorded trajectories to executable scene geometry, AirPlayground also enables analysis of how offline prediction errors relate to closed-loop outcomes. Specifically, AirPlayground makes three main contributions. 

(i) Dense flight experience paired with executable reconstructions of real-world environments. 

We pair dense flight demonstrations with photorealistic reconstructions and aligned navigation and collision geometry for policy learning and matched closed-loop execution. 

(ii) A unified benchmark across navigation policy paradigms. Our extensible API supports shared-data policy training and unified closed-loop evaluation across three navigation settings, while preserving each policy’s native representations and inference structure through adapters. 

(iii) Cross-policy evaluation and deployment diagnosis. We benchmark seven baselines and an-alyze offline predictions and closed-loop behavior. These studies show that offline accuracy alone does not fully explain deployment performance and examine how execution horizons and scene ge-ometry affect navigation, demonstrating the benchmark’s value beyond performance comparisons. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Aerial Navigation Datasets. Prior work has expanded navigation-level supervision from con-strained simulated settings toward larger urban environments, richer tasks, and broader scene cov-erage (Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b; Lee et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026c). More recent efforts 2scale collection through automated planning and expert trajectory generation (Low et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Adang et al., 2025). Other efforts collect fine-grained flight demonstrations for reactive control and long-horizon navigation (Wang et al., 2025a; Zheng et al., 2026). Despite these ad-vances, temporally dense flight demonstrations remain limited in scale and environmental coverage, constraining the continuous control experience for training diverse navigation policies. 

Reconstructed Environments and Navigation Benchmarks. Recent embodied-navigation re-search uses 3D Gaussian Splatting to reconstruct photorealistic real-world environments, providing a real-to-sim bridge for policy learning and evaluation (Chhablani et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a; Low et al., 2025; Quach et al., 2024). To support collision-aware navigation, these representations are augmented with metric geometry, collision models, and navigation structures, enabling interactive navigation across reconstructed environments (Miao et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b; Xia et al., 2026). Aerial benchmarks also pair 3DGS environments with geometry for collision checking and evaluation: OpenFly spans 18 scenes from multiple rendering sources, while HUGE provides four reconstructed outdoor environments (Gao et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026). These plat-forms establish important foundations, but broader coverage of reconstructed indoor, urban outdoor, and vegetation environments is still needed for systematic cross-scene policy evaluation. 

Navigation Policy Evaluation. Offline evaluation measures prediction errors against expert demonstrations, while interactive aerial benchmarks assess task success and path efficiency through closed-loop rollouts (Xue et al., 2026; Lou et al., 2026). Studies in autonomous driving and robot manipulation examine this mismatch, including the predictive value of evaluation metrics and task-critical errors (Wang et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). This mismatch also depends on how predic-tions are executed: work on action-chunking policies investigates execution horizons (Zhao et al., 2026), while studies of aerial agents examine how execution mechanisms affect flight outcomes (Xu et al., 2026). However, a systematic understanding of the offline-to-closed-loop gap in aerial nav-igation remains limited. By pairing flight demonstrations with executable worlds, AirPlayground supports trajectory replay and policy evaluation in corresponding scenes, providing an experimental basis for tracing offline predictions to their closed-loop consequences. 

## 3 AIR PLAYGROUND BENCHMARK 

This section describes how AirPlayground builds executable environments and dense expert flight experience from real-world captures, and supports policy training and evaluation through a unified interface. Figure 2 summarizes the construction pipeline, from scene capture and reconstruction to scene-adaptive task generation, expert flight collection, and the resulting shared flight corpus. 3.1 SCENE COLLECTION 

Capture hardware. An operator walks each site at a steady pace carrying an XGRIDS Lixel P1 PortalCam, a handheld scanner that pairs a wide-field LiDAR with four synchronized cameras, two fisheye and two forward-facing. A single walk-through therefore yields both metric geometry and multi-view imagery of the scene (Appendix A.1). 

Scene selection and diversity. We choose environments spanning three settings with distinct nav-igation demands: indoor spaces such as rooms and corridors, with confined free space and clutter; 

urban outdoor sites such as campuses, streets and landmarks, with regular structures; and vegeta-tion -rich environments such as parks, gardens and terrain, with irregular geometry and diffuse ob-stacle boundaries. Scenes are captured on site or adapted from publicly available assets (Xia et al., 2026), then processed into a common representation (Sec. 3.2). The corpus contains 94 scenes: 18 indoor, 48 urban outdoor and 28 vegetation. Navigable regions total 75,657 m 2, ranging from 14 to 6,674 m 2 per scene (median 567 m 2; mean 805 m 2), spanning over two orders of magnitude. 3.2 EXECUTABLE SCENE RECONSTRUCTION

Fisheye images are undis-torted, and a 3D Gaussian Splatting model (Kerbl et al., 2023) is initialized from the point cloud and optimized under depth supervision on the registered images. The pipeline detects dynamic regions and segments them into masks, and the masked regions are held out of the optimization so that tran-sient content does not enter the reconstruction. Additional assets are brought into the same form, so every scene ends up as a 3DGS asset paired with a metric point cloud in one frame. 

Executable geometry. A visual asset shows what a scene looks like, but not where a drone may fly or what it would strike. We derive both from the point cloud. A 2D occupancy grid defines the free space for task sampling and path planning. It covers a flight-relevant height band, is restricted to a navigable polygon annotated once per scene, and dilates occupied cells by a per-scene inflation radius so paths keep a margin from obstacles. A 3D clearance field, precomputed once per scene without inflation, answers collision and clearance queries against the robot radius and preserves the vertical structure the 2D grid collapses; GPU queries batch this checking without physics-engine contacts. The 3DGS asset, planning grid, and clearance field share one coordinate frame, so visual observations, task planning, and collision checking refer to the same scene (Appendix A.1). 3.3 SCENE -A DAPTIVE DEMONSTRATION GENERATION 

Scene-adaptive task generation. A scene specification records each world’s occupancy and in-flation settings, navigable polygon and flight altitude. Within the navigable region, the pipeline samples start–goal pairs subject to a minimum separation and a clearance margin from obstacles, keeps a pair only if a planned path connects its endpoints, and resamples otherwise. The initial heading is randomized around the goal direction, so the flown trajectory is not fully determined by the planned path. Where a scene offers little natural obstacle interaction, the pipeline adds box-shaped obstacles in free space to diversify trajectories; the released corpus records their count per episode (Appendix A.2). 4Closed-loop demonstration collection. For each task, A* plans a reference path over the inflated occupancy grid, and greedy line-of-sight shortcutting then shortens it. The expert follows this path in closed loop: at each 50 Hz control step, a waypoint-tracking expert reads the drone’s current simulated state, steers toward the active waypoint, and outputs planar body-frame velocities and a yaw-rate command. Each step synchronously records forward RGB, the UAV state, a local occu-pancy patch and the executed action. Only successful episodes are retained. Worlds are collected independently and in parallel across GPUs, targeting 1,000 successful demonstrations per scene and yielding the corpus reported in Sec. 3.5. 3.4 UNIFIED CROSS -P OLICY API Navigation policies differ in observation histories, training targets, and inference and execution mechanisms. AirPlayground handles these differences through training and stateful control adapters, allowing policies to reuse the same flight corpus and evaluation pipeline. The API supports point-goal, image-goal, and goal-free navigation. Given a trajectory τ = {(ot, s t, g t, a t)}Tt=1 , a training adapter converts it into the inputs and su-pervision of policy m, (xm, ym) = Tm(τ ), constructing observation histories and policy-specific targets, including single-step actions, action chunks, waypoints, or future observations for predic-tive models. Future observations serve as training supervision, not deployment inputs. For goal-free policies, the adapter withholds the goal input gt and the relative-goal entries of the state st.At deployment, a stateful control adapter maps policy outputs, including actions selected by model-based planning, to body-frame velocity commands, ut = Cm(ˆ ymt , s t; hmt ) with ut = ( vx, v y , ˙ψ),where hmt tracks cached predictions and progress through the current plan. It executes each pol-icy’s outputs on its own execution schedule, standardizing task inputs and control semantics while preserving method-specific prediction and planning mechanisms. New policies implement these adapters while reusing the task generator, simulator, and evaluator. 3.5 RESOURCE STATISTICS AND VALIDATION 

Corpus scale and schema. AirPlayground contains 94 executable worlds and 94,000 successful expert trajectories, 1,000 per world. Recorded at 50 Hz, the trajectories comprise 46,530,557 syn-chronized RGB–state–action samples, 495 per trajectory on average. They amount to roughly 259 hours and 1,800 km of flight, stored in 5.9 TB. Each sample pairs a forward 256 × 256 RGB im-age with a 13-dimensional UAV state that embeds the relative goal, a local occupancy patch, and a continuous body-frame control action (ux, u y , ω ) whose translational components are normalized (Appendix A.5). This schema lets every policy formulation in Sec. 4 read from the same trajectories. 

Comparison with existing aerial resources. Table 1 compares environment representations, scale, and flight supervision, which differ in granularity and coverage beyond raw trajectory counts. CityNav and OpenFly provide discrete navigation supervision, with OpenFly providing waypoints. UAV-Flow records flight at 5 Hz in real footage, and FLIGHT at 10 Hz in synthetic environments, but neither uses 3DGS reconstructions. HUGE-Bench pairs 3DGS with collision geometry across four scenes. AirPlayground combines 94 executable worlds with 94K trajectories and continuous body-frame control supervision at 50 Hz. 

Trajectory statistics and validation. Planned paths average 19.2 m (median 13.5 m, 95th per-centile 55.6 m, maximum 235.1 m), and episodes last 9.9 s on average (median 7.1 s, 95th percentile 27.6 s). Per-scene frame counts range from 43,665 to 2,023,166. All 94,000 released trajectories are successful expert demonstrations, and every one of them is accounted for in storage, with no missing and no orphaned files. All baselines in Sec. 4 use this corpus for training or adaptation and offline evaluation (Appendix A.5). 

Visual appearance and geometry. We evaluate rendering fidelity across 20 scenes with 10 refer-ence images per scene, using camera poses estimated from image correspondences and mesh depth. At 1280 × 720 resolution, the evaluation yields an average PSNR of 19.19 dB, SSIM of 0.655, and LPIPS (AlexNet) of 0.223. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate AirPlayground through three central research questions: (1) How do aerial navigation policies perform across diverse reconstructed environments under a shared evaluation protocol, and how well do they generalize to unseen scenes? (2) How does increasing available training experi-ence affect closed-loop performance? (3) What explains the observed discrepancies between offline prediction accuracy and closed-loop navigation performance? 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmark. We split 94 environments into 70 training, 8 validation, and 16 unseen test scenes, with all three categories in each split. Each scene has 1,000 trajectories; training scenes reserve 100 for seen offline evaluation and use 900 for learning. Closed-loop evaluation uses 50 fixed episodes in each of 16 training scenes and all 16 unseen test scenes: 800 per split and 1,600 per policy and setting. Split scores weight scenes equally. Offline-to-closed-loop comparisons use matched scenes. 

Task protocol. Policies navigate at a fixed flight height using forward RGB, flight state, and task-specific goal inputs, and output planar body-frame velocities and yaw rate. PointGoal supplies a relative target; ImageGoal uses successive views along an A* reference route; Goal-free withholds the target, which is retained only for evaluation. Success requires reaching within 0.45 m of the goal without a safety violation. Episodes end on success, collision, leaving the navigable region, or a 100 s timeout; collision checking uses a 0.12 m body radius. 

Baselines. We benchmark seven baselines: CNN-BC, ACT (Zhao et al., 2023), Diffusion Policy (DP) (Chi et al., 2025), ViNT (Shah et al., 2023), OmniVLA (Hirose et al., 2025), NWM (Bar et al., 2025), and NavMorph (Yao et al., 2025). Unified evaluation reveals complementary policy strengths. Evaluating different policies on the same flight corpus and task reveals complementary strengths across metrics and scene splits (Table 2). NWM achieves the highest seen-scene SR and A ∗-SPL with the lowest CR, while Nav-Morph attains the lowest OB. On unseen scenes, OmniVLA leads in SR and OB, NWM in A ∗-SPL, and NavMorph in CR. Comparable success rates can mask execution behaviors: NWM and Om-niVLA achieve seen-scene SRs of 50.0% and 47.5%, yet NWM exhibits lower control variation and path turning (ControlVar 0.003; Turn/m 0.120) than OmniVLA (0.540; 3.919). Across navigation settings, ImageGoal with successive image subgoals yields higher SR than PointGoal for three of seven baselines, while withholding goal input lowers SR for all seven (Table 10), revealing policy-dependent responses to goal guidance. Figure 4 illustrates paths and outcomes in three scenes. 

Better offline prediction does not always yield better navigation. CNN-BC achieves the lowest seen-scene ADE and FDE but only 21.0% SR. Averaging seen and unseen scores for each policy, ADE and FDE have Spearman correlations of ρ = 0 .29 with SR, opposite to the expected negative relationship. Their correlations with CR are weak ( ρ = 0 .18 ), while FDE correlates negatively with OB ( ρ = −0.43 ), contrary to expectation. Thus, lower displacement error does not consistently indi-cate better closed-loop performance, motivating examination of deployment-relevant error structure.

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduce AirPlayground, a unified benchmark for aerial navigation that pairs dense flight expe-rience and scene geometry with executable real-world reconstructions. By adapting shared trajec-tories and unifying deployment controls, our cross-policy API enables different policy formulations to learn from the same flight corpus and undergo standardized evaluation across diverse environ-ments. We benchmark seven baselines spanning four policy paradigms across three navigation set-tings in seen and unseen scenes, while providing a unified interface for additional policies. Beyond measuring task performance, our analyses reveal an offline-to-closed-loop mismatch involving er-ror structure, execution protocols, and interactions with scene geometry. Together, these analyses demonstrate how AirPlayground combines standardized performance comparison with deployment diagnosis in the corresponding environments. We will publicly release the flight dataset, cross-policy API, and benchmark tools. We hope AirPlayground will serve as a foundation for aerial policy learn-ing and evaluation, helping researchers train policies on dense flight experience, diagnose behavior in executable worlds, and explore geometry- and collision-aware learning. 9AI U SE STATEMENT

## A DEMONSTRATION COLLECTION PROTOCOL 

AirPlayground generates navigation demonstrations by sampling tasks in reconstructed scenes, plan-ning reference paths, and executing a feedback controller in the simulator. The collection unit is a scene-specific PointGoal episode. Each episode contains observations and control commands from the executed flight, together with the task configuration and reference path. The following descrip-tion details the base collection pipeline and separately documents its optional diversity mechanisms. Scene assets and frames. A scene ships as a USD package holding the renderable asset together with a triangle mesh of the same reconstruction, alongside a separate point-cloud file. Because all of these come from one reconstruction, they already share a frame; the collection code applies a single fixed map transform to bring them into the simulation frame, and no pairwise registration between appearance and geometry is performed. The packaged mesh is exposed to the environment as a ray-cast target for depth queries. The clearance field described below is what supplies collision geometry, and it is derived from the point cloud rather than from that mesh. 

Occupancy construction. Point-cloud coordinates are scaled, rotated, and translated into the sim-ulation frame. Points within the configured height interval are projected onto a horizontal grid with cell width δ. A cell is initially occupied when its point count reaches the configured threshold; an optional vertical-extent threshold further filters the projected points. Small connected compo-nents are removed before obstacle inflation. Occupied cells are then dilated by a discrete circular neighborhood with radius ⌈rinf /δ ⌉, where rinf is the configured inflation distance. Finally, cells whose centers lie outside the navigable polygon are marked occupied. The resulting grid defines the feasible domain for sampling and path planning. The planning grid describes horizontal traversability. A feasible grid path is followed by an actual closed-loop rollout before the episode is accepted, so grid feasibility and successful execution are separate checks in the collection procedure. 

Collision distance field. Because the planning grid is horizontal, it cannot distinguish an obstacle that is dangerous only at ceiling height from one that is dangerous at flight height. A separate 3D clearance field supplies that distinction. We first rebuild the footprint above without inflation, then voxelize the height-filtered, polygon-cropped point cloud on the same horizontal grid extended by a vertical axis at the same resolution, and keep only voxels whose (x, y ) column survives the cleaned footprint. This retains the two-dimensional denoising while recovering height structure, which a 3D connected-component filter applied directly would fragment. A Euclidean distance transform over the remaining free voxels, scaled by the voxel size, yields the field, stored with its origin and resolution. The bake applies no inflation. At run time a query point is mapped to its voxel by flooring, the stored clearance is read in a vectorized gather on the simulation device, and the robot radius is subtracted; a non-positive result signals collision. Points outside the baked volume are treated as free, and departures from the scene are handled separately by out-of-bounds and height checks. The bake reuses the simulator’s map transform so that the clearance field, the planning grid and the rendered asset share one frame; this correspondence has to be maintained whenever a scene overrides that transform. 13 A.2 TASK SAMPLING AND INITIALIZATION 

Candidate generation. The sampler draws horizontal positions within the polygon’s bounding box and rejects positions outside the polygon. For endpoint sampling, it further dilates the planning occupancy by an endpoint-clearance margin. Candidate positions are mapped to nearby free cells in this sampling mask and checked again for polygon membership. Duplicate cells are removed, yielding a set of up to N candidate locations. 

Start–goal selection. For candidate locations {ci}Ni=1 , the sampler constructs eligible unordered pairs 

E = {(i, j ) : i < j, ∥ci − cj ∥2 ≥ max( dmin , 2δ)} , (1) where dmin is the minimum start–goal separation. A pair is selected uniformly from this set, and its start and goal roles are randomly exchanged. If sampling or subsequent planning fails, the collector resamples the task. The path-length distribution this produces is measured on the collected episodes (Sec. 3.5). 

Initial pose. The robot and goal share an episode-specific initial height drawn from the scene configuration. Planning operates in the horizontal plane, and the action interface supplies horizontal body-frame velocity and yaw rate, with zero commanded body-frame vertical velocity. The robot is initialized at rest. In the default goal-facing initialization, its yaw points toward the goal with a uniformly sampled offset in [−45 ◦, 45 ◦]. This produces variation in the initial heading while keeping the task endpoints fixed. 

Virtual obstacle placement. Beyond the geometry recovered from the scene itself, episodes may include virtual box obstacles placed in the free space of the planning grid. The number of virtual obstacles is fixed within a collection run. Given the sampled start and goal, their centers are drawn by rejection sampling over free grid cells: a candidate is accepted when its box footprint, expanded by a planning clearance, contains no occupied cells, and when it lies at least a configured distance from the start, from the goal, and from every previously accepted virtual obstacle. Sampling stops once the requested number of centers is accepted; an episode whose virtual obstacles cannot be placed within the attempt budget is rejected and resampled. Each accepted obstacle is assigned a random yaw, and its footprint, expanded by a planning clearance, is written into the planning occupancy grid before path search. Episode metadata records the number of placed obstacles and their positions; in the released corpus, 65,000 of the 94,000 episodes (69.1%) contain virtual obstacles. A.3 REFERENCE PATH PLANNING 

A* searches an eight-connected occupancy grid. Horizontal and vertical moves have unit cost, diagonal moves have cost √2, and the heuristic is Euclidean distance in grid coordinates. A diagonal transition is allowed only when both adjacent axial cells are free, preventing corner cutting during grid search. Endpoints are associated with nearby free cells within a bounded snapping radius, and the search runs under a bounded expansion budget; a failed endpoint association, an exhausted budget, or an unsuccessful search causes the task to be rejected. After search, a greedy shortcutting pass reduces the number of intermediate waypoints. Starting from the current anchor, it selects the farthest subsequent waypoint whose connecting segment passes the grid’s discrete free-space check, and repeats this operation until the goal is reached. A.6 BEHAVIOR DIVERSITY MECHANISMS 

Beyond the base episodes, the collector provides mechanisms that extend task and behavior cover-age. All of them record into the same observation–action schema as base episodes. 

Obstacle- and corner-aware speed. An optional multiplier reduces speed according to nearby occupied cells and upcoming path bends. Clearance is measured from the center of the local occu-pancy patch to the closest occupied cell in a forward-facing region that includes a small rear margin. A clipped clearance ratio determines one speed factor. A second factor decreases as the robot ap-proaches a waypoint whose turning angle reaches a specified threshold. The controller uses the smaller factor, bounded below by the configured minimum speed fraction, together with the base goal slowdown. 16 Near-obstacle starts. The hard-start sampler selects free cells from a configurable clearance band around obstacles. It samples a separate feasible goal and varies the initial heading using uniform, obstacle-facing, or goal-facing modes. The sampled start must remain inside the scene polygon, and the resulting task must pass the same planning and execution checks as a base episode. 

Perturbation and recovery. During selected episodes, the collector temporarily holds an earlier action or adds a bounded horizontal command bias. After the disturbance ends, it replans from the current robot position to the original goal and resumes waypoint tracking. The action array stores the command actually selected during the disturbance, while event metadata additionally records the contemporaneous expert commands, event timing, and replanning outcome. These fields distinguish disturbance segments from subsequent expert recovery when constructing training samples. 

Planning and camera variation. An episode-level inflation offset changes the planning margin, while the local occupancy observation continues to use the base geometry representation. This keeps observation geometry consistent across planning-margin draws. Camera variation changes horizontal field of view at episode boundaries while retaining the square RGB resolution. The collector records the drawn settings and available runtime camera metadata for later analysis. A.7 COLLECTION ACROSS SCENES 

Scene collection processes operate independently, each loading its own scene assets, sampling tasks, executing the expert, and writing episode shards. An orchestration layer runs one process per scene and packs several processes onto each GPU, so that a single launch fills every available GPU up to a configured per-GPU process count and queues the remaining scenes. Collection writes to fast local storage and promotes a scene to durable network storage only once its shard metadata reports the requested episode count and every archive opens as a valid file, which keeps the step loop off the network-storage path. A reference count derived from already-collected scenes sets the per-scene target, a resumable staging layout supports continuation after interruptions, and a watchdog frees the GPU slot of a collector that reports a large, configurable number of attempts without a success. Failed task attempts are resampled within a scene, and process-level failures do not halt the remaining queue. The common recording schema permits outputs from independently collected scenes to be aggregated for policy training.

Goal specifications. AirPlayground’s unified API supports baseline training and evaluation with PointGoal, ImageGoal, and Goal-free conditioning. PointGoal supplies a relative target. Here, ImageGoal uses successive A ∗ subgoal views, denoted Image Subgoal ; the API also supports a single terminal goal image. Goal-free withholds goal input and relative-goal state entries, retaining the target only for evaluation. Table 10 reports navigation under these guidance settings. Image subgoals are rendered at the episode’s flight height along the A ∗ route, sampled every 0.5 m starting 0.5 m from the start. For route length L, the final view is placed at arc length max( L/ 2, L −

1 m) . Each view faces the next subgoal, and the final view faces the task goal. Before each policy step, the subgoal index advances monotonically while the current subgoal is within 3 m Euclidean distance of the drone, stopping at the first farther subgoal or the final view. The selected image is supplied through the goal-image interface. This provides route guidance without requiring arrival at every subgoal. Shared evaluation tasks. The full three-setting protocol uses the same 16 seen and 16 unseen scenes and 50 start–goal episodes per scene for each supported policy–setting pair (800 per split; 1,600 total). Task completion and safety. These results demonstrate the API’s support for learning and execut-ing policies under different forms of goal guidance. Image Subgoal yields higher SR than PointGoal for DP, ViNT, and OmniVLA, but lower SR for the other four methods. Successive visual subgoals provide intermediate route guidance, yet their benefits depend on the policy. Withholding goal input lowers SR for all seven methods.
