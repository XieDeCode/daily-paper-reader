## Abstract 

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become a standard representation for real-time novel-view synthesis. Yet, its quality–efficiency trade-off still relies heavily on adaptive density control. Existing cloning, splitting, and pruning rules use proxy signals, such as position gradients, opacity, and image-space error. Although effective in practice, these signals do not directly answer the structural question behind density control: where should new Gaussians be allocated to reduce the multi-view reconstruction loss, and which existing primitives can be pruned with minimal penalty. To address this, we propose first-variation birth–death control for 3DGS, a principled approach that replaces heuristic structural decisions with a variational score derived from the reconstruction loss. At each structural update, we freeze the current Gaussian population, attach auxiliary mass coordinates to each primitive, and differentiate the multiview loss with respect to these coordinates. We demonstrate that this score is a coordinate of the first variation of the Splat Regression Model, giving a rigorous local descent surrogate for birth-death moves: high-score Gaussians are selected as birth sites, while low-score, low-opacity Gaussians are selected for removal. Across standard 3DGS benchmarks, integrating this score into 3DGS-MCMC yields state-of-the-art reconstruction quality and outperforms existing methods, delivering the most significant gains under tight Gaussian budgets. Ultimately, our work provides compelling evidence that gradient-based variational metrics offer a principled alternative to proxy heuristics in 3DGS. 

## 1 Introduction 

High-quality photorealistic scene modeling from images is a fundamental task in computer vision and graphics, with applications spanning immersive rendering, robotics, digital humans, and geometric reconstruction [4, 38]. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [15] has emerged as a fast explicit radiance-field representation that models scenes as anisotropic Gaussian primitives and renders them by differentiable rasterization. Its speed and explicitness have led to rapid extensions in dynamic rendering, anti-aliasing, generative 3D content, and SLAM [9, 11, 14, 24, 27, 28, 32, 34, 37]. Despite this progress, 3DGS training still relies on heuristic adaptive density control (ADC). Training a 3DGS model interleaves two distinct optimization mechanisms. Continuous optimization adjusts the parameters (position, covariance, color) of existing Gaussians to fit the image data. Structural control, meanwhile, changes the population itself by creating or destroying primitives. In standard 3DGS, these population updates rely on indirect proxies: high accumulated position gradients trigger cloning or splitting, while low opacity triggers pruning [15]. These signals correlate with regions needing refinement, but they do not directly measure how a discrete birth or death event affects the photometric objective. As a result, primitive placement is sensitive to hand-tuned thresholds.

The model frequently misses high-frequency details, creates floaters or needle-like artifacts, and wastes redundant Gaussians to regions that are already well covered [10, 25, 36]. The cost is most visible under tight primitive budgets, where redundant placement directly competes with missing detail [2, 17, 19]. While recent methods improve these signals through pixel-aware gradients, error-guided insertion, and budgeted densification [2, 22, 36], they remain mathematically decoupled from the empirical risk. The Splat Regression Model (SRM) framework [6] provides a rigorous lens to understand this limitation: it models splat fitting as optimization over mixing measures structured by Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao (WFR) geometry. Under this view, standard 3DGS successfully performs continuous 

transport (moving Gaussian parameters via gradient descent), but completely ignores the WFR mass 

component when deciding where to spawn or destroy primitives. Because disconnected heuristics govern these mass perturbations, a fundamental allocation problem remains unresolved: How can we identify which discrete primitives to kill, and where to spawn new ones guided by a first-order descent surrogate for the multiview reconstruction loss? 

Contributions. In this work, we propose reframing 3DGS structural updates from a collection of proxy heuristics into a formal component of empirical risk minimization. Motivated by the WFR geometry, we formulate structural control as a finite-particle birth–death process. At each control step, we freeze the continuous scene parameters and attach an auxiliary mass coordinate to each Gaussian. Differentiating the multiview reconstruction risk with respect to these coordinates yields a per-primitive first-variation score. This score is the negative directional derivative of the objective, evaluated at the frozen parameter state. It mathematically predicts the first-order descent achieved by applying an infinitesimal mass perturbation to that specific primitive. We then use score gaps to guide the birth-death process: capacity is reclaimed by killing low-score, low-opacity Gaussians and reborn at high-score sites. This provides a criterion for birth–death decisions. Because finite structural updates lack strict continuous descent guarantees, we execute them via the state transitions of 3DGS-MCMC [17]. This work bridges the theoretical view of splat fitting as mixing-measure optimization with practical 3DGS training. Our contributions are: • We replace heuristic proxy signals with a first-variation score for 3DGS structural control, directly tying primitive allocation to the multiview empirical risk. • We theoretically justify this framework by discretizing the SRM first variation, and by proving that restricting death to low-opacity Gaussians bounds the immediate rendering disturbance. • We demonstrate that variational allocation outperforms proxy heuristics when representation capacity is constrained. Across standard benchmark datasets, our method achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction quality against matched 3DGS-MCMC and recent budget-aware baselines. 

1.1 Related Work Adaptive density control and densification. The original 3DGS framework couples continuous photometric optimization with adaptive density control: accumulated position-gradient statistics trigger cloning or splitting, while low opacity triggers pruning [15]. A massive body of recent work improves this controller. Revising Densification and Pixel-GS replace or reweight the growth criterion using pixel-level error or pixel-aware gradients [25, 36], while AbsGS and Improved ADC refine gradient aggregation, threshold schedules, and pruning rules [10, 33]. Other methods manage the primitive budget explicitly through resource-aware schedules, progressive propagation, or high-error insertion rules [2, 5, 22]. While these methods engineer better proxy signals or insertion heuristics, our work diverges fundamentally: we abandon proxies in favor of the structural derivative. By explicitly differentiating the multiview risk with respect to Gaussian mass, we formalize capacity allocation as a direct objective rather than a heuristic byproduct. 

Relocation, MCMC, and compact representations. Several methods improve 3DGS efficiency by pruning redundant Gaussians or compressing attributes post-training [8, 12, 20, 23], while others avoid incremental growth entirely by relying on dense initializations [19]. The closest backend to our method is 3DGS-MCMC [17], which rewrites densification and pruning as relocation/addition transitions. Our approach adopts these discrete transitions from 3DGS-MCMC, but completely replaces its structural selection statistic. Rather than relying on opacity-sampling to blindly select 2relocation targets, we use our first-variation score to predict where released capacity is most likely to reduce the reconstruction risk under a local first-order model. 

Variational splat models and first variation. The Splat Regression Model (SRM) formalizes splat models as optimization over mixing measures, deriving Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flows and explicit first-variation characterizations of mass rescaling [6]; related birth–death discretizations also appear in sampling theory [21]. However, while SRM establishes the continuous, measure-level WFR geometry, it does not prescribe a finite, budget-constrained training algorithm for discrete rasterizers. Our contribution bridges this gap: we derive a concrete, renderer-level mass-coordinate score and a finite score-gap birth–death controller, making this continuous variational geometry actionable for practical 3DGS training.

Limitations and future work. While the first-variation score provides a principled surrogate for mass allocation, several natural frontiers remain. First, as a scalar mass derivative evaluated at existing sites, qi dictates where to allocate capacity rather than parameterizing the optimal geometric displacement of split children; extending this to joint geometry-mass proposal scoring is an exciting direction. Second, because the score tracks photometric residuals, severe cross-view inconsistencies (e.g., auto-exposure in unconstrained captures) can occasionally misguide allocation at high capacities (Appendices G.2 and E). Coupling variational density control with exposure- or uncertainty-aware models presents a promising solution. Finally, adaptive score schedules that decay update frequency as geometry stabilizes could further amortize the auxiliary backward-pass overhead (Appendix G.3). 

## 5 Conclusion 

We reframed 3DGS structural control from a collection of heuristics into a formal mass-allocation problem. By evaluating the first variation of the multiview reconstruction risk, we replaced heuristic cloning and pruning with a principled birth–death controller. Our experiments expose the fundamental limitation of standard 3DGS: proxy signals succeed when excess capacity hides their inefficiencies. When the primitive budget is constrained, querying the objective’s mass derivative is essential to preserve complex geometry and thin structures. This demonstrates that 3DGS structural updates belong in the same rigorous empirical risk minimization framework as continuous parameters. Moving forward, future compact and dynamic radiance fields can bypass heuristic bottlenecks by letting the reconstruction risk govern structural growth.

G.1 Mass score and opacity-gradient parameterization 

The proposed mass-coordinate score is closely related to standard opacity gradients, but it provides a more appropriate coordinate space for structural allocation. In the frozen score pass, the auxiliary coordinate mi modulates the renderer through the opacity footprint: ai(x; m) = miαiGi(x). This scaling by αi explains the practical stability of the mass-coordinate score. The raw opacity derivative ∂ bFt/∂α i evaluates a scalar perturbation without accounting for the primitive’s current presence in the scene. In practice, raw gradients often spike on nearly invisible "floaters" attempting to fit high-frequency noise. By measuring the relative rescaling of the primitive’s mass, the factor αi

acts as an objective-level confidence weight. A nearly transparent primitive with a noisy gradient is correctly suppressed, forcing the controller to prioritize structurally established Gaussians for birth sites. For death decisions, the logic inverts cleanly: we first filter for safe slots via the low-opacity pool, then use the score to cull the slots whose removal will least impact the reconstruction loss. 17 Crucially, the mass score also bypasses a hidden artifact in standard continuous optimization. 3DGS typically optimizes opacity via a logit ζi, where αi = σ(ζi). The gradient is therefore: 

∂ bFt

∂ζ i

= αi(1 − αi) ∂ bFt

∂α i

.

Because of the (1 − αi) factor, logit gradients saturate and vanish for highly opaque, solid Gaussians (αi ≈ 1). If a density controller relied on the optimizer’s internal logit gradient to identify structural candidates, it would inherently penalize the most established, reliable primitives in the scene. Because our mass score qi is derived directly from the linear mass coordinate mi, it measures the true first-order descent direction, isolated from the saturation artifacts of the activation function. We therefore use qi exclusively for discrete birth–death selection, leaving continuous opacity optimization to the inherited 3DGS-MCMC backend.
