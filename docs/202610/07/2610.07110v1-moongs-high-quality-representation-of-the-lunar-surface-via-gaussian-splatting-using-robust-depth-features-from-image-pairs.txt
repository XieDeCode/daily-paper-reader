# MoonGS: High-quality Representation of the Lunar Surface via Gaussian Splatting Using Robust Depth Features from Image Pairs 

Yun Jiang, Bo Zheng, Yingying Zhang, Xueming Xiao, Tao Hu, Hutao Cui, Zhiguo Meng, Ke Gao, Yang Gao, and Meibao Yao 

Abstract —High-quality 3D reconstruction of lunar terrain from sparse rover images is indispensable for autonomous lunar exploration, but remains challenging because viewpoint overlap is insufficient, surface textures are weak, and data volume is limited. We propose MoonGS, the first feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting framework tailored to lunar scenes. Given only two input images, MoonGS predicts pixel-aligned Gaussian primitives in a single forward pass and renders photorealistic novel views without any per-scene optimization. MoonGS (i) adopts an adaptable backbone design that seamlessly integrates advanced vision foundation models to extract robust depth features; (ii) integrates semantic priors in two manners: merging semantic cues with visual features to refine Gaussian parameter estimation, and adopting a semantic ranking loss that regularizes background depth; (iii) employs an entropy-guided heuristic resampling strategy to augment sparse observations by selecting the most informative distant viewpoints with negligible overhead. Experiments on the LuSNAR benchmark and our synthetic weak-texture MoonBlenderdataset show that MoonGS surpasses state-of-the-art feed-forward NeRF/3DGS baselines by +4.9 dB PSNR, +0.29 SSIM, and ↓40% LPIPS while maintaining sub-second inference. Furthermore, we validate the broad applicability of our framework by demonstrating that it effectively leverages state-of-the-art backbones, including VGGT, to significantly boost perfor-mance. Qualitative evaluations on Chang’e mission imagery also show best visual quality among compared methods, indicating robustness on real lunar data. The source code and dataset are publicly available at https://github.com/InRobots/MoonBlender. 

Index Terms —Lunar Exploration, Novel View Synthesis, Feed-forward Gaussian Splatting, Vision Foundation Model.

Classical dense 3D reconstruction methods, including point cloud or voxel representations [5]–[7], typically depend on local image similarity metrics. However, since 3DGS requires training from scratch for each new scene, it currently cannot meet real-time application requirements. Additionally, it requires densely captured images to ensure quality, making it unsuitable for common scenarios with extremely sparse observations in lunar exploration missions. Despite recent progress, lunar scene reconstruction still faces several distinct and unresolved challenges: (i) limited viewpoint overlap during exploration often invalidates tra-ditional epipolar constraints, hindering accurate cross-view feature matching; (ii) lunar background regions exhibit weaker textures compared to foreground objects, complicating depth estimation and geometric reconstruction due to sparse visual cues; (iii) mission-imposed resource constraints severely limit the number and quality of observations, further challenging the accurate representation of lunar surfaces. To overcome these critical challenges, we propose MoonGS, a feed-forward 3DGS framework that synthesizes novel views from sparse lunar surface images and rich semantic informa-tion with high fidelity, while achieving real-time performance, as illustrated in Fig. 2. To begin with, (i) MoonGS leverages robust cross-view depth features provided by an off-the-shelf vision foundation model to achieve a reliable scene repre-sentation, particularly in regions lacking sufficient viewpoint overlap. Moreover, (ii) to further enhance performance, we integrate semantic information into our method in two critical ways: firstly, semantic priors are used to improve the predic-tion of Gaussian parameters, resulting in more stable scene geometry; secondly, a semantic ranking loss is introduced to regularize depth estimation in weak-textured background regions (e.g., the sky), significantly reducing geometric errors. In addition, (iii) we propose a heuristic resampling strategy to effectively augment sparse lunar surface observations, sub-stantially enhancing the fidelity of synthesized novel views. Meanwhile, (iv) we also introduce a new synthetic lunar dataset as an extension to existing lunar datasets, deliberately designed with extremely sparse sky textures to specifically evaluate reconstruction performance under weakly textured conditions. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: 1) We introduce MoonGS, a feed-forward 3DGS frame-work specifically designed for the Moon, which repre-sents scenes using Gaussian primitives and enables high-quality synthesis of novel views from sparse viewpoints in real time. 2) We fully exploit robust depth features from an off-the-shelf vision foundation model to address insufficient viewpoint overlap, effectively integrate semantic priors and a semantic ranking loss to handle unreliable depth estimation in weakly textured regions, and adopt aheuristic resampling strategy to overcome data sparsity. 3) We provide a dataset named MoonBlender as an ex-tension of existing lunar datasets, specifically designed to evaluate the performance of scene representation methods under weakly textured conditions. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Celestial Surface Reconstruction Based on Radiance Fields 

Reconstructing celestial surface environments is critical for tasks such as geological analysis [12] and navigation simula-tion [13], providing essential data for robotic perception [14]– [21] and mission planning [22], [23]. Early approaches to novel view synthesis were based on light fields, initially using densely sampled data [24], [25] and subsequently allowing un-structured capture [26]. The advent of structure-from-motion (SfM) [27] opened the door to synthesizing novel views from photo collections, by estimating a sparse point cloud during camera calibration. Building on SfM, multi-view stereo (MVS) techniques [28] enabled the development of full 3D reconstruction pipelines and further advanced view synthesis algorithms [29], [30]. These methods typically reproject and blend input images based on the recovered geometry, produc-ing high-quality results in many scenarios. However, they can still suffer from unreconstructed regions or artifacts caused by inaccuracies in the MVS-generated geometry. Neural ra-diance field methods achieve higher fidelity and have shown promise in novel view synthesis, 3D reconstruction, and pose estimation [31]. Mildenhall et al. [8] encode a scene as an MLP-based implicit function and use volume rendering, which is computationally expensive due to extensive ray sampling. Subsequent techniques [32], [33] accelerate radiance field methods, and among these, 3DGS [9] employs Gaussian primi-tives with a rasterization approach to circumvent ray sampling, reconstructing scenes from dense posed images. Radiance fields have also been applied to celestial environments for high-fidelity visualization. For instance, MaRF [34] recon-structs Martian landscapes from NASA’s PDS, and Zhang et al. [35] extends neural radiance fields to lunar surface scenes. However, existing approaches often suffer from data sparsity and lack real-time performance. To address these issues in lunar applications, we format sparse lunar data as image pairs and design a specialized model architecture to exploit this sparsity. B. Feed-forward Radiance Fields 

To enable real-time reconstruction, researchers have ex-plored feed-forward radiance field methods that reconstruct a scene from a single forward pass. PixelNeRF [11] uses one or few input images to form an entire scene, integrating pixel features into rendering and showing that radiance fields can be learned from sparse views. Du et al. [36] adopt epipolar sam-pling and a lighter renderer for better results, and MuRF [10] constructs a cone volume of the target view, achieving strong performance via convolutional neural networks. However, all these NeRF-based methods rely on ray sampling, resulting in slower performance. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [9] is significantly faster in both training and rendering. Consequently, many sparse-view reconstruction methods have adopted 3DGS. For instance, Splatter Image [37] employs U-Net to directly infer pixel-wise Gaussian parameters from a single view for object-level reconstruction, while PixelSplat [1] learns epipolar attention from two related views to predict depth distributions. However, ambiguous feature-to-depth mappings can misposition Gaus-sians and blur the final images. MVSplat [2] constructs a cost volume for more precise geometry, and MVSGaussian [38] similarly uses cost volumes for depth estimation. Yet, end-to-end MVS approaches struggle with limited overlap in planetary data and are not tailored to these scenarios. In contrast, MoonGS leverages a vision foundation model for depth-related features, inferring reliable depth even with sparse observations. It also uses rich lunar semantics as prior knowledge and employs a heuristic resampling strategy. These techniques yield superior reconstruction results for lunar scenes. 

C. Key Components of Feed-forward 3DGS 

In feed-forward 3DGS models, depth estimation is the most critical component, as it determines the positions of the Gaussians, which are more difficult to optimize compared with other parameters. Certain carefully designed approaches help obtain reliable depth estimates. Notably, pixelSplat [1] employs a combination of DINOv2 [39] and an epipolar transformer for feature extraction, whereas MVSplat [2] uses UniMatch’s [40] CNN and transformer modules to derive features. These components share the characteristic of first using the same encoder to acquire image encodings, followed by modules with cross-attention to decode and obtain cross-view features. However, DINOv2 is independent of depth estimation. UniMatch relies on matching between two input images to estimate depth, making it less robust in face of occlusions or other challenging scenarios. In contrast, vision foundation models such as DUSt3R [41] and VGGT [42] are pretrained on large-scale datasets and improve overall depth consistency and accuracy by generating pointmaps for each view. The features extracted from the foundational model fur-ther enhance performance in depth-related downstream tasks, such as image matching [43]. III. M ETHODS

The key to our approach lies in how we infer more reliable depth, enabling precise determination of the Gaussian primitives’ positions, even in non-overlapping areas. A. Motivations and Preliminaries 

Motivations. As lunar exploration missions progress, integrat-ing 3DGS for scene reconstruction plays a crucial role in augmenting robotic perception. Due to the inherently sparse nature of lunar data, applying standard methods often leads to artifacts or even reconstruction failures. Although several 3DGS-based methods exist for sparse-view reconstruction, they still face various difficulties. As illustrated in Fig.4, data collected by a forward-moving lunar rover encounter multiple issues: invalid epipolar constraints in non-overlapping regions, insufficient observations for regions far from the camera, and ambiguous depth in low texture sky areas, among others. Although state-of-the-art approaches—such as pixelSplat [1] and MVSplat [2]—mitigate these challenges only partially, key problems remain unsolved. In this work, we aim to address these limitations more effectively.

C. Robust Depth Features and Cost Volume 

Our method focuses on how to obtain effective depth features to precisely locate Gaussian primitives. As a vision foundation model, DUSt3R [41] is designed to extract cross-view features from a pair of input images. These features are subsequently decoded to generate a depth-related pointmap. Since DUSt3R has been pre-trained on a large amount of data, the features it extracts perform well in downstream tasks. 5

Therefore, we treat the features extracted from DUSt3R as robust depth features, which constitute a core component of our approach. 

Robust depth features extraction. As shown in Fig.3, the vision foundation model (specifically DUSt3R) consists of two identical branches with the same structure, including an image encoder and decoder. Each branch only inputs one of the paired images. The pair of images are first encoded in a Siamese manner by the same weight-sharing ViT encoder [44], resulting in two token representations, F1 and F2:

F1 = Encoder (I1), F 2 = Encoder (I2). (5) The decoder then performs joint reasoning on these inter-mediate features. Through self-attention and cross-attention mechanisms, the decoder effectively processes the view-related information embedded in these features. Specifically, the self-attention module enables the features to focus on other impor-tant regions within the current view, while the cross-attention module allows the features to attend to relevant regions across different views. This stacked structure, in which information is continuously exchanged, ensures that both branches acquire cross-view features after decoding. This process is a common technique in many advanced architectures and is crucial for reconstructing an entire scene using image pairs. D. Integrating Semantics for Enhanced Performance 

The lunar scenes encompass rich semantic information, which can originate from expert annotations or be obtained through segmentation models such as SAM [48]. Semantic information serves as a highly effective prior in many tasks [49], [50], inspiring us to design methods that incorporate se-mantics to improve model performance in lunar reconstruction tasks. 

Semantic fusion. Compared to indoor scenes, the semantics of objects in lunar scenes better reflect their characteristics. For instance, different categories of cups typically exhibit markedly distinct texture characteristics. Conversely, rocks of various shapes often possess similar texture features, and rock surfaces differ significantly from those of lunar regolith, which is shown in Fig.5. Semantic information must also account for certain special cases, such as scenarios where the texture differences between lunar regolith and impact craters are minimal. This inspires us to incorporate semantic information as a prior into the model to improve the accuracy of predicting Gaussian parameters. Specifically, we concatenate the one-hot encoded semantic information with the original features to predict the depth map and Gaussian parameters. When 6

semantics are unreliable or absent, we gate out the fusion branch and use only appearance- and geometry-based features. Rock (from view 1) Mountain Lunar regolith Rock (from view 2)

These specific details help us to better understand the working parts of the method and allow repro-ducing the entire experiment. A. Datasets 

We used the LuSNAR [55] lunar sequence dataset that is compatible with our training pipeline. We also created a MoonBlender dataset with weak textures and more complex rock distributions, and we added qualitative evaluations on Chang’e mission imagery to verify effectiveness on real-world data. 

LuSNAR is a multi-scene multi-label lunar benchmark dataset built for lunar exploration missions, using Unreal Engine 4 [56] as a simulator for its project. And for our architecture, it has serialized data, and each image contains real semantic labels and camera poses, which can provide the most basic guarantee for our task. In addition, LuSNAR has multiple scenes, allowing us to verify our algorithm in different scenes. It includes five semantic categories, namely sky, lunar regolith, rocks, impact craters, and mountains. The semantic pixel ratio is shown in Fig.7. The most important visual sense is that the rocks contained are relatively large irregular rocks that are relatively few. The sky belongs to the weak texture area and has a large pixel ratio. 

MoonBlender is a lunar dataset we synthesized using Blender [57], which not only extends the LuSNAR dataset but also serves to validate the problem of weak texture, sparse view reconstruction. Real captures can produce frames with a large sky background. Panorama sweep patterns, narrow field of view optics, and operations constrained by illumination and thermal windows limit pointing range and increase the inci-dence of large sky background frames [58]. To quantify how often this occurs, we randomly sampled 96 images from the CE4 TCAM dataset, which contains Terrain Camera images captured by the Chang’e 4 lander during surface operations. 43 images had no sky background, and 53 images contained sky background. Among the images with sky background, the mean background pixel ratio was 42.11% as shown in Fig.7. B. Experimental Settings 

Comparison methods. We compared methods for feed-forward synthesis of novel views from sparse views, including NeRF and 3DGS. Du et al. [36] is a light field-based rendering method that can aggregate epipolar features from the Trans-former structure. MuRF [10] is a sparse view reconstruction method based on NeRF. PixelSplat [1] and MVSplat [2] are advanced feed-forward methods based on 3DGS. Our method is mainly improved from MVSplat. These methods were compared qualitatively and quantitatively with ours. 

Evaluation metrics and training losses. For image rendering quality, we used standard image quality metrics to quantita-tively evaluate and compare our method with other methods: pixel-level PSNR, patch-level SSIM [62], and feature-level LPIPS [63]. Since our training was trained and evaluated through the standard explicit camera model training pipeline, we could directly compare with the real ground truth. In addition, to more clearly illustrate the change in the overall resource consumption of the model after using the vision foundation model, we evaluated our resource consumption such as the number of trainable parameters and inference time to illustrate the increased resource consumption compared with other models. In addition, we also qualitatively evaluated the accuracy of the depth estimation of the model. It is common and effective to use MSE and LPIPS loss functions with weights 1 and 0.05, respectively during the training stage. Ranking loss with weight 0.01 was used in our study to regularize weakly textured regions. In general, the loss can be described by the following formula: 

Ltotal = LMSE + 0 .05 × L LPIPS + 0 .01 × L Ranking . (12) 

Hardware and implementation details. All timings were measured on NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 (Q6000) GPUs. Training used 4 GPUs with a global batch size of 16 (4 images per GPU) at an image resolution of 256 × 256 . We trained for 30,000 steps. We then fine-tuned for 500 steps with the ranking loss enabled to better regularize weak textured regions. Evaluation and inference were performed on a single Q6000. Unless otherwise stated, inference timings correspond to a batch size of 1. We optimized all models with Adam using a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4.

This section presents the scope of MoonGS, states its limitations, and outlines future work. First, several modules in our pipeline use semantics. When semantics are unavailable, we activate a fallback mechanism that preserves stability. In future work, we plan to reduce this dependence by incorporating priors that are easier to obtain. These include edge cues, regionness cues, uncertainty-aware pseudo-labels, and surface normals. Second, MoonGS focuses on pair-based feed-forward novel view synthesis. We specifically adopt this setting to address the challenge of sparse viewpoints in lunar exploration. However, the framework can be extended to incorporate three or more views, as the underlying vision foundation model supports multi-image inputs and the cost volume formulation gener-alizes to pairwise matching. In each forward pass the number of Gaussian primitives equals the number of input pixels, so the per-pass memory footprint depends on image resolution and per-primitive state rather than terrain size. Larger maps can be obtained by fusing outputs from multiple inferences of our model. For the fused maps, exploring hierarchical tiled representations and spatially adaptive splatting can improve memory efficiency and scalability, which we leave to future work. Third, although inference is fast, the network is not yet lightweight enough for constrained devices. We will explore model distillation, pruning, and quantization to obtain smaller models while preserving accuracy. Fourth, we keep the default backbone (DUSt3R) fixed during training. With limited lunar data, this choice improves stability and data efficiency. As larger and more diverse datasets become available, we plan to partially unfreeze the backbone and fine-tune selected layers to further improve adaptation. Fifth, as validated by the VGGT integration, our framework exhibits robust applicability to advanced backbone architec-tures. By incorporating stronger priors while freezing the foundation model, we ensure parameter efficiency without sacrificing performance. Future work will leverage this adapt-able design to integrate next-generation foundation models, a strategy that guarantees our method continues to capitalize on the rapid advancements in underlying feature representations. Finally, the current pipeline assumes known relative camera poses as input. In future work, we will investigate pose-free variants to broaden the applicability of our method. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced MoonGS, a feed-forward 3DGS method tailored to lunar environments. It aimed to improve the per-ception ability of robots on lunar terrains by addressing the problems of invalid epipolar constraints, ambiguous depth in weakly textured regions and insufficient data observations. First, we employed robust depth features provided by the vision foundation model to achieve better Gaussian positions, particularly in regions with limited overlapping views. Second, we incorporated lunar semantic priors into the model, which significantly enhanced its overall performance. To address ambiguous depth in regions with weak texture, we proposed a semantic-based ranking loss for depth regularization. In addition, we designed a heuristic resampling strategy to further improve the rendering quality. Finally, to facilitate a compre-hensive evaluation under challenging conditions, we introduce MoonBlender, a dataset extending existing lunar datasets and specifically designed to assess reconstruction performance in weakly textured regions. We also reported qualitative results on real Chang’e imagery, where MoonGS delivered higher-fidelity reconstructions and showed robustness under varying illumination. Experimental results showed that compared to state-of-the-art methods [1], [2], MoonGS achieved higher-quality reconstructions on two lunar datasets, even in non-overlapping or weakly textured regions. Furthermore, exper-iments with the advanced VGGT [42] backbone validated the broad applicability of our framework, demonstrating its capacity to seamlessly leverage state-of-the-art architectures for enhanced performance.
