# Return-to-Home Feasible Micro-Aerial Vehicle Exploration for 3D Gaussian Splatting Reconstruction 

Prajit KrisshnaKumar, Fan Yang, and Koichiro Niinuma 

Abstract — Micro aerial vehicles (MAVs) enable rapid indoor 3D reconstruction for inspection and time-critical situational awareness, but must operate under strict flight-time budgets and safety constraints that require explicit return-to-home (RTH) feasibility with a conservative margin. We present a topology-first active reconstruction framework that decouples high-fidelity rendering from navigation. A dense 3D Gaus-sian Splatting (3DGS) map is optimized as the reconstruc-tion target, while a lightweight Truncated Signed Distance Field (TSDF)/occupancy scaffold supports conservative collision checking and online construction of a sparse 3D Voronoi skeleton roadmap. Since directly extracting roadmaps from TSDF geometry can be unstable under noisy and incomplete online fusion, we validate nodes and edges using carved free-space consistency and dense visibility checks, which suppress behind-wall phantom structure and stabilize planning. View-points are selected on the roadmap using a flight-time-budget-aware objective and a lightweight receding-horizon lookahead to improve non-myopic exploration behavior. We evaluate on photorealistic indoor simulation benchmarks (ReplicaCAD, Gibson, and HM3D), reporting reconstruction coverage/error, RTH success, and computational cost. Results demonstrate improved or competitive performance relative to recent GS-based baselines under identical flight-time budgets. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Micro aerial vehicles (MAVs) are increasingly attractive for indoor search-and-rescue (SAR) and first-responder mis-sions because they can quickly enter confined or hazardous spaces and provide situational awareness before humans are deployed [1]. In these environments, autonomy must handle cluttered, GPS-denied navigation through narrow doorways and corridors under strict flight-time limits. If the MAV exhausts its battery deep inside a structure, safe recovery is not guaranteed and the mission may fail prematurely. Consequently, a central operational requirement is return-to-home (RTH) feasibility. In multi-room indoor layouts, return cost is highly non-Euclidean, and straight-line distance can severely underestimate the true time required to navigate around walls and through doorways. Therefore, viewpoint selection must trade off expected reconstruction gain against travel time while maintaining a conservative safety margin for possible interruptions during the return flight (Fig. 1). Practical autonomy therefore typically requires an auxiliary geometric scaffold for safe navigation and feasibility reasoning. While recent work has begun coupling Gaussian splatting with active viewpoint planning, reliably integrating high-fidelity mapping with an execution-ready navigation abstrac-tion under budget constraints in multi-room indoor scenes remains challenging. ActiveGS [3] uses a voxel scaffold to represent unexplored space and guide exploration, which can become costly to maintain and query as the explored 3D vol-ume grows. ActiveSplat [4] enables efficient planning using a planar topological abstraction, but this 2D workspace does not explicitly capture the 3D connectivity and occlusions that arise in indoor flight. To bridge these gaps, we take a topology-first approach that decouples photorealistic reconstruction from conserva-tive navigation reasoning. The MAV incrementally optimizes a dense 3DGS map as the reconstruction output, while simultaneously fusing the same posed RGB-D observations into a lightweight TSDF and occupancy grid that provide explicit surfaces and conservative free-space evidence for planning. Since online TSDF geometry is incomplete early in exploration and can contain holes and transient artifacts, directly extracting a roadmap can introduce behind-wall phantom nodes that waste flight time and jeopardize safe return. We therefore apply conservative validation using carved free-space consistency and dense visibility checks to keep the roadmap stable for MAV execution. On the resulting roadmap, we formulate viewpoint actions as (node, yaw) pairs and select goals using a budget-aware objective that trades off information gain against shortest-path travel time while enforcing an explicit RTH safety margin. Finally, to reduce myopic oscillations, we use a lightweight two-step receding-horizon selection, motivated by receding-horizon next-best-view planning for aerial exploration [6]. a) Autonomous Exploration and Informative Path Plan-ning: Informative Path Planning (IPP) selects sensing actions to maximize information gain or coverage under constraints such as time, range, or energy [7]. In unknown environments, IPP is commonly solved online using an evolving map so that viewpoint selection adapts to newly observed structure. For UAV exploration in large 3D spaces, scalable representations and clearance-aware planning are important; examples in-clude low-memory mapping for exploration [8] and Voronoi-guided planning with adaptive layering [9]. Complementary work targets robust state estimation and globally consistent geometric mapping, such as GO-SLAM [10], Voxgraph [11], and Kimera [12]. 

b) High-Fidelity Scene Reconstruction: NeRF-based methods produce high-quality view synthesis but rely on ex-pensive volume rendering, which limits real-time closed-loop use. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2] provides an efficient alternative via explicit Gaussian primitives and fast rasteri-zation, enabling interactive rendering and online refinement and motivating NBV-style active reconstruction [6], [13]. Recent systems estimate view utility from renderability or uncertainty cues [14]–[16]. Since 3DGS is rendering-centric and does not provide conservative free-space semantics, many approaches pair it with auxiliary geometric scaffolds or sparse navigation abstractions for safe planning, such as ActiveGS [3], ActiveSplat [4], and neural-map exploration methods [17]. 

c) UAV-Based Indoor Reconstruction and Unresolved Gaps: Indoor UAV reconstruction systems study exploration and mapping under practical sensing and execution con-straints [18], [19]. Recent 3DGS-focused planners also target quadrotor autonomy, including GS-Planner [20] and HGS-Planner [21]. Despite progress, robust autonomous recon-struction in cluttered multi-room indoor scenes remains chal-lenging. Dense voxel scaffolds for unknown-space reasoning can become expensive as explored volume grows, while planar abstractions can miss 3D connectivity and occlusions important for indoor flight. Moreover, return-to-home feasi-bility is inherently non-Euclidean indoors because return cost must respect doors and walls, and sparse roadmaps extracted from online fusion can become unstable early in exploration without conservative validation. These limitations motivate hybrid architectures that optimize 3DGS as the reconstruc-tion target while planning and safety reasoning are performed on a lightweight geometric scaffold under explicit budget and RTH feasibility constraints. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Method Overview 

We address edge-assisted budgeted autonomous explo-ration for high-fidelity indoor reconstruction using an RGB-D equipped micro aerial vehicle. A fundamental challenge in active radiance-field mapping is that representations op-timized for photorealistic rendering (e.g., 3DGS) lack the explicit, conservative free-space semantics required for safe navigation. To resolve this, our framework (Fig. 2) adopts a hybrid perception–action loop. Specifically, the system: (i) incrementally optimizes a dense 3DGS map as the primary reconstruction target, while concurrently (ii) fusing depth into a lightweight TSDF to serve as a geometric scaffold for extracting a sparse 3D topological roadmap. Using this roadmap, (iii) a budget-aware planner selects and executes next-best viewpoints to maximize coverage while enforcing return-to-home (RTH) feasibility.

C. 3D Voronoi Roadmap Construction 

We construct a sparse, topologically meaningful abstrac-tion of the environment: a 3D Voronoi roadmap whose nodes approximate the medial axis of free space and whose edges maximize obstacle clearance. To ensure the roadmap remains stable under continuous map updates, we employ a pipeline that fuses depth into a TSDF, extracts temporally smoothed obstacle sites, computes a 3D Voronoi tessellation, and rigorously filters the resulting graph using explicit volumetric carving. 

1) Volumetric Fusion and Space Carving: At each timestep k, the drone captures a posed RGB-D observation, denoted as the tuple (Ik, D k, TW ←C,k ). Here, Ik is the RGB image, Dk is the aligned depth map, and TW ←C,k ∈ SE (3) 

is the rigid camera pose mapping points from the camera frame C to the world frame W . Using the camera’s known intrinsic parameters, we back-project each pixel u from the depth map Dk to obtain a 3D point cloud Pk in world coordinates: 

pW (u) = TW ←C,k π−1 u, D k(u), (3) where π−1(·) denotes the pinhole back-projection function. Often, these depth measurements contain noise, incom-plete information, floating outliers, and view-dependent missing regions. To obtain a stable geometric estimate suit-able for topology extraction, we apply voxel-grid downsam-pling to remove duplicates and reduce fusion cost. We then integrate this filtered point cloud into a sparse volumetric TSDF [22]. The TSDF stores a signed distance ϕ(x) and weight w(x) per voxel center x, which are updated iteratively as a weighted average of the truncated distance observations. In addition to the TSDF surface, we maintain a conser-vative notion of free versus occupied space for fast wall-crossing checks [23]. For each depth endpoint pW,k (u), we ray-march from the camera origin ok to the endpoint. Voxels along the ray before the endpoint are marked free , and the endpoint voxel is marked occupied . Let F be the set of free voxels and O the set of occupied voxels at a fixed resolution 

r (e.g., r = 0 .20 m). Because noisy depth endpoints can temporarily mark a previously carved free voxel as occupied (creating phantom geometry or "floaters"), we define an effective occupied set using Oeff = O \ F which prevents these “disputed” voxels from incorrectly blocking doorways or corridors. 

2) Obstacle Sites: We extract a surface mesh from the TSDF zero-level set using marching cubes [24]. The Voronoi tessellation is computed from a discrete set of generator points; if these generators change slightly between cycles, Voronoi vertices and edges can change disproportionately. To reduce this sensitivity, we convert the extracted mesh into a persistent set of obstacle sites S = {(si, ni)} with associated surface normals. Sites are stored in a coarse voxel-hash map and updated with an exponential moving average (EMA):

4) Graph Sparsification: Even if two vertices are individ-ually valid, the straight segment between them may cross a wall. For each candidate edge e = ( va, vb) we sample points along the segment following x(t) = (1 − t)va + tvb, t ∈

(0 , 1) and reject the edge if either (i) any sample violates a minimum clearance threshold with respect to the obstacle sites, or (ii) any sample voxel falls in Oeff (dense occupancy traversal). Finally, we remove tiny disconnected components, sparsify the graph (e.g., contracting near-collinear degree-2 chains and merging nearby nodes to bound computation). 

5) Free-Space Node Injection and Bridging: The raw 3D Voronoi graph can under-sample wide-open regions because Voronoi vertices tend to concentrate near obstacle boundaries. To improve coverage and connectivity, we inject additional waypoint nodes directly from the carved free-space grid F and connect them to the existing roadmap using the same clearance and Oeff collision checks as for standard edges. This augmentation fills coverage gaps in open areas and provides safe “bridges” between weakly connected components. Figure 3 visualizes the carved free space and the resulting augmented roadmap. The output of this stage is a sparse, collision-checked roadmap G =(V, E) with per-node attributes (position, clearance), which is then used for viewpoint scoring and planning in subsequent sections. 

D. Viewpoint Selection 

At each planning iteration, the planner requests a snapshot of the current 3D Voronoi roadmap G = ( V, E) from the previous step. Each node n ∈ V stores its 3D position pn and per-node attributes (e.g., clearance), and the snapshot also provides two per-view gain tensors: GainV scores garea (n, h )

and frontier-surface scores gsurf (n, h ) for a discrete set of yaw headings h ∈ { 0, . . . , H − 1} (we use H = 8 headings, spaced by 45 ◦). An action or the next waypoint is defined as 

a = ( n, h ): moving to node n and observing with heading 

h.

1) Return-to-Home Feasibility Filters: To ensure safe recovery, we enforce a hard return-to-home (RTH) constraint: a candidate goal is admissible only if the MAV can reach it and still return to the takeoff location with a conservative time reserve. We compute shortest-path travel times on the roadmap G using two Dijkstra passes [26]: cgo (n) is the travel time from the current state to node n, and chome (n) is the travel time from n back to the home node. Let B denote the total flight-time budget and let t be the elapsed flight time. We define the remaining budget Trem = B − t and require 

cgo (n) + chome (n) + Tres ≤ Trem , (7) where Tres is a fixed safety reserve (time margin). 

2) Frontier Indicator: Each candidate node is assigned a frontier strength score derived from two signals: (i) an 

obstacle-frontier count, computed by counting surface points that have only been observed from far camera ranges in the neighborhood of the node, and (ii) a free-space frontier 

count, computed from classic occupancy-grid frontiers within a search radius [27]. 

3) Information Gain: We have primarily two scoring methods to calculate the potential information gain for se-lecting an action a. Area discovery gain, termed as GainV (garea (n, h )) estimates how much new space a depth camera would reveal from action a = ( n, h ) by raycasting a camera frustum from pn at heading h through a voxel grid that clas-sifies cells as free, occupied, or unknown. It is essentially the number of unknown voxels observed before occlusion. While this term prioritizes new area, another term, frontier surface gain ( gsurf (n, h ) ) improves the reconstruction quality. This is achieved through clustering frontier obstacle points and scoring clusters that (i) are visible from the node (line-of-sight in the occupancy grid) and (ii) fall inside the view’s field of view.

IV. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

To validate the proposed exploration framework, we eval-uate our MAV-based active reconstruction system in photo-realistic indoor simulation to quantify reconstruction quality, safety (return-to-home feasibility), and computational cost. 

A. Simulation Setup and Execution Model 

All experiments are conducted in the Habitat simulator, which provides photorealistic indoor environments, synchro-nized RGB-D observations, and a simulation clock. We evaluate on datasets with increasing layout complexity: (i) compact apartment-style scenes (ReplicaCAD), and (ii) multi-room residential scenes with narrow doorways and long corridors (Gibson and HM3D). For Gibson [28] we use the same scene set as prior work [29], for Replica-CAD [30] we select three one-bedroom apartments, and for HM3D [31] we evaluate on two scenes (HaxA7YrQdEC, TEEsavR23oF). We target an edge-assisted MAV setting: the MAV executes sensing and waypoint control, while 3DGS optimization, roadmap construction, and viewpoint selection run on a workstation/base-station computer equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU and an AMD Ryzen 5950 CPU with 32 GB RAM. Planned actions are defined on the roadmap as a target node position and a discrete yaw heading (Sec. III-D.5). The MAV executes the resulting roadmap path with a simple velocity controller (position P-control with a yaw-rate controller). To improve robustness in cluttered indoor layouts, we use a depth-triggered safety check that performs short recovery behaviors (e.g., skipping a blocked intermediate waypoint or aborting the current segment) be-fore replanning. Prior to exploration, the MAV performs an initialization scan (a slow 360 ◦ yaw rotation) at the start location to stabilize the initial map for all methods. We use Habitat ground-truth poses and simulated RGB-D sensing to isolate viewpoint planning and mapping; thus, the reported RTH feasibility is map-relative and does not model VIO drift, real sensor noise, or aerodynamic disturbances. Fig. 4 qualitatively illustrates this behavior: the MAV rapidly expands coverage early in the mission and then shifts toward refining remaining incomplete surfaces as the flight-time budget depletes. Ac-tiveGS is designed to optimize surface reconstruction quality using Gaussian confidence rather than explicitly maximizing global coverage. In multi-room layouts this can bias selection toward local refinement and limit traversal into new rooms under a fixed flight-time budget, resulting in lower coverage in our evaluation.

D. Computational Cost Analysis 

We profile wall-clock overhead for mapping and planning, excluding these from the simulated flight budget. During flight, our dense 3DGS backend continuously processes RGB-D frames at ∼4 FPS. Between these continuous way-point traversals, our intermittent replanning cycles average 

0.93 s for planning and 0.84 s for mapping. While Ac-tiveGS has a lower per-step planning latency ( 0.42 s), its myopic actions force excessive replanning, accumulating massive per-episode totals ( 81 .5 s planning, 90 .1 s mapping). In contrast, our hybrid architecture decouples 3DGS from a lightweight topological scaffold. This enables long-range waypoint actions that collect data continuously, drastically reducing the required number of replanning interventions and yielding highly efficient episodic totals of just 16 .4 sfor planning and 12 .8 s for mapping. V. C ONCLUSION 

We present a budget-aware active reconstruction frame-work for micro aerial vehicles that targets high-fidelity 3DGS while explicitly enforcing return-to-home (RTH) feasibility. Our approach decouples rendering from navigation by op-timizing a dense 3DGS map as the reconstruction output and maintaining a lightweight TSDF/occupancy scaffold for conservative collision checking and online construction of a sparse 3D Voronoi skeleton roadmap. Viewpoints are selected as (node, yaw) actions using a budget-adaptive objective and a lightweight two-step receding-horizon looka-head to reduce myopic decisions under limited flight time. Across photorealistic indoor simulation benchmarks, our method achieves strong reconstruction performance while maintaining safe recovery behavior, attaining high RTH success and outperforming a 2D/topological Voronoi-based planner and a surface-centric 3DGS baseline in mean cov-erage and/or reconstruction error under identical flight-time budgets. Limitations include a simple depth-triggered reac-tive safety policy that can waste time in cluttered scenes and a roadmap update procedure that rebuilds the 3D Voronoi graph periodically, contributing non-trivial computation.
