Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting achieves excellent visual quality with real-time rendering, but at the scale of entire cities it does not fit:
a trained model carries millions of primitives and gigabytes of memory, and real-time rendering at high quality on a consumer
GPU remains out of reach. We introduce Budgeted-GS, a post-hoc method that turns any trained 3DGS model into a factoring
tree, a multi-resolution hierarchy of moment-matched aggregates. After a construction pass of a few seconds, a single quality
parameter then selects, for each view, the level of detail that fits the memory of the target device, so the same city-scale model
serves GPUs with widely different memory capacities. When a new scene is to be trained, the same theory applies: instead
of growing a full-sized model and compressing it afterwards, budget-centered training first measures how many primitives the
scene needs and then trains the model directly at that size, avoiding the wasted effort of optimizing primitives that are later
discarded. Both methods are grounded in a measurable capacity floor, a budget–error law derived from optimal transport in
phase space; selection rules certified by recent covering theorems decide which primitives are redundant. The floor answers
how many primitives a scene actually needs and how many can safely be given up. We validate the floor on 13 public scenes
under a preregistered protocol, and exercise both methods from object scenes to an official city capture, rendering it at native
1920×1080, full SH, in real time on one consumer GPU.

1. Introduction
Real-time radiance-field rendering of captured scenes is now
routine at object and room scale. 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [KKLD23] represents a scene as a set of anisotropic
Gaussians rendered by projective splatting with depth-sorted α-
compositing. At city scale it does not fit: a trained model carries
millions of primitives and gigabytes of memory, and real-time ren-
dering at high quality on a consumer GPU remains out of reach.
The obstacles are structural. At training time the standard recipe is
to densify a large model and then compress it; the primitives are
optimized for a different capacity, and the time spent on the re-
moved half is wasted. At deploy time the only degradation mode
offered by streaming renderers such as Gaussian Point Splatting
(GPS [RPWM26]) is random subsampling. And beneath both sits
an unanswered question: nobody can say what the right budget is.
The question “how many primitives do I need here?” is answered
by habit, because no principled budget–error floor has been avail-
able either at training or deployment time.
This paper answers it with a rate–distortion theory of rendering
primitives: a trained model is a point on a rate–distortion curve;
its primitive count is its budget, its error a function of that budget.
The theory has two mathematical halves. The capacity floor, de-
rived from optimal transport in phase space (position × direction,
where rendering primitives and renderers are both natural objects),
says that if the scene’s phase-space measure has dps-dimensional
support, the minimal achievable error with N primitives falls as
N−2/dps (Zador’s law). The collapse license comes from the 2025
resolution of the three-dimensional Kakeya set conjecture [WZ25]:
the Wang–Zahl union-volume estimate, in weighted (continuous-
opacity) form, certifies that wherever Gaussians locally over-cover
space, they merge into few representatives with bounded volumet-
ric loss. The floor tells a budget what it can achieve; the license tells
it where redundancy lives and what may be merged or dropped.
The floor and the license are operationalized as two methods.

2. Related Work
Rendering large-scale scenes. Every classic representation solved
scale with its own multiresolution answer. Points: surfel splat-
ting [PZvBG00], QSplat’s sphere hierarchy [RL00], and Potree’s
out-of-core octree generation [SOW21] stream large point clouds at
interactive rates. Meshes: progressive meshes [Hop96] introduced
view-dependent refinement, and Nanite [Kar21] virtualizes microp-
olygon geometry in production engines. Voxels: sparse voxel oc-
trees [LK10] ray-march gigavoxel volumes. What these systems
share, a coarse-to-fine hierarchy carrying a per-frame budget, is ex-
actly what a raw 3DGS model [KKLD23] lacks: it is a flat set of
primitives with no hierarchy at all.
Data structures for large-scale 3DGS scenes. Inside the 3DGS
world, the standard answer is a spatial structure coupled to training.
Octree-GS builds an octree of level-of-detail clusters [RJL∗24];
Scaffold-GS anchors Gaussians on a sparse grid [LYX∗24]; mul-
tiresolution hash grids organize NeRF-side training [MESK22];
CityGaussian partitions the scene into blocks with distance-
based LOD [LGL∗24], following the block decomposition of
Block-NeRF [TWN∗22]; hierarchical 3DGS trains a chunk pyra-
mid [KMK∗24]; concurrent efforts pursue out-of-core streaming
hierarchies [WKR∗26] and LOD-aware training [KRM∗25]; GPS
streams an octree with distance shells [RPWM26] (our cross-
renderer comparisons use it, Section 5). These structures carry the
indexing, but the budget lives in the training pipeline: levels and
blocks are fixed when training ends. Our factoring tree is built
post-hoc from any model, selects per (leaf, scale-band) instead of
per block or level, and grounds the selection in measurable redun-
dancy and pixel-footprint criteria (Section 5); Section 6.4 com-
pares directly against Octree-GS and CityGaussianV2’s runtime
LOD [LLM∗25].
Quality and memory control for large-scale 3DGS scenes.
Once a model exists, a large family controls its size: MCMC-
based densification [KRS∗24] and regularization-based prun-
ing [MGK∗24] budget during training; LightGaussian [FWW∗24],

3. Overview
Budgeted-GS applies one budget discipline at every stage of a
scene’s life cycle (Fig. 1). The capacity floor (Section 4) gives, for
any scene, the minimal achievable rendering error at a primitive
count N; it is stated as theorems and measured per scene with a
preregistered protocol (Section 6.1). Two methods spend the bud-
get where the floor says. Budget-Centered Training (Section 5.1)
reads the floor’s knee N∗off the measured curve and trains the
densifier directly at that budget. Factoring LOD (Section 5) con-
verts any trained model into a factoring tree whose one-parameter
pixel-footprint scheduler selects, per view, exactly the working set
that fits the memory at hand. Section 6 reports the experiments. The
supplementary material carries the proofs.
4. An optimal-transport theory for 3D Gaussian primitives
Why should the budget be a design variable, and how should it be
spent? On real data, reconstruction error falls steeply as the budget
grows, then flattens into a shallow floor. This section develops a
theory that predicts where that floor lies: from primitives and ren-
dering at the bottom, through construction and capacity, up to the
selection rules on top. Concretely, for a quality target Q, the theory
yields (i) the minimal primitive count a scene requires, (ii) the re-
dundant subset of its primitives, and (iii) the allocation of a fixed
budget across a city.
4.1. Rendering as optimal transport in phase space
Prior work has employed optimal transport in 3DGS only in narrow,
task-specific roles, including Wasserstein losses between Gaussian
mixtures [SLZ∗25], radial-optimal-transport fitting of structured
Gaussians [ZCY∗24], and capacity control in multi-view styliza-
tion [WYW∗26]. To our knowledge, we are the first to formulate
rendering as optimal transport in phase space.

5. Budgeted-GS: training and deployment
The floor and the selection rules are operationalized two ways: at
training time, budget-centered training grows the model the floor
prescribes (Section 5.1); at deployment time, the factoring tree and
its pixel-footprint scheduler serve, per view, exactly the working
set that fits the memory at hand (Sections 5.2 and 5.3).
Both halves of our system follow from one observation: a trained
model is a redundant, multi-scale arrangement of oriented boxes
(each primitive occupies a box of half-extents h = 3σ weighted by
its opacity), and budget control means removing that redundancy
without visible loss. Three principles govern every selection rule in
this paper; they are the operational form of the theorems stated in
Section 4.
The first principle is the collapse license: a group of primitives
that locally over-covers space, many overlapping boxes of compa-
rable scale in one region, can be merged into a few representatives
with bounded volumetric loss (Theorem 7; the moment-matching
counterpart of the optimal-atom prediction, Section 4). This li-
censes the moment-matched aggregates of the factoring tree and
opacity-ranked pruning inside redundancy-flagged regions.
The second is scale truncation: a primitive whose projected foot-
print is below the pixel scale can be dropped or collapsed with
bounded image error (Lemma 8; the U2 projection-error regime).
This licenses the pixel-footprint threshold t = c·d/ f of the sched-
uler and the stopping rule that freezes density control once pruning
stops paying for itself.
The third is budget allocation: under a fixed primitive bud-
get, coarser scale bands deserve exponentially more budget than
finer ones (Corollary 10; the Kjsj/dj law); the same footprint
rule instantiates this allocation per view. The three principles are
grounded in recent union-volume estimates for unions of convex
sets [WZ25] and classical adaptive-approximation rates [DeV98];
measured error rates on synthetic scenes match the prediction
within measurement noise.
5.1. Budget-centered training
The floor answers “how many primitives do I need at quality Q?”,
and the answer is also a training prescription. Traditional pipelines
train a large model and compress it: the primitives are optimized
for a different capacity, and the training time spent on the removed
half is wasted. BCT instead locates the knee N∗from the measured
curve (Fig. 2; the budget with the largest steepest-window slope,
Section 6.1), then trains the MCMC densifier directly at cap = N∗:
the initial primitive count is set to a fixed initial fraction of N∗and
the refine-stop fraction to a second, both scaled by the measured
redundancy θwaste = Nopt/Nfull.
Standard 3DGS densification/pruning is driven by positional gra-
dients and opacity. We keep densification but replace the prun-
ing signal by the θ redundancy map (the collapse license; supple-
mentary material): θ is computed per region from the local fac-
toring of the model: regions with high local multiplicity (low θ)
are over-covered and are exactly where primitives can be removed
with the smallest approximation loss; pruning inside θ-flagged re-
gions removes primitives by opacity. A rate-based stopping crite-
rion freezes density control once validation improvement falls be-
low ε = 0.5 dB.

w∗= W/|V ∗| ∈[0,1] (Fig. 3c). Colors enter as opacity-weighted
first moments of the SH coefficients (DC and higher bands sepa-
rately).
The internal nodes of the octree receive the same treatment
bottom-up, so every (node, band) has one aggregate plank. The raw
primitives are retained in Morton order for the finest tier. Construc-
tion on multi-million-primitive scenes takes seconds on one GPU,
and the aggregate representation is 119× smaller than the input
(supplementary material).
5.3. The pixel-footprint scheduler
At render time, for camera position p and focal length f (pixels),
each (leaf, band) with mean scale ¯s and centroid distance d is as-
signed to one of three tiers by the threshold t = c·d/ f:

6. Experiments
We validate the theory and both methods end to end: the floor itself
across 13 public scenes, then Budget-Centered Training, then Fac-
toring LOD from object scenes to city scale, closing with ablations
and efficiency.
6.1. The floor, measured across 13 scenes
The floor is measured with a preregistered protocol (criteria frozen
before data collection; all verdicts frozen before reading results; 3
fixed seeds; JSON certificates at every stage, supplementary mate-
rial): on each scene, MCMC training at cap 2 × 106 followed by
opacity-prune to N and fine-tune yields the empirical floor curve
R(N) at 9 budgets; the preregistered floor-intactness test asks that
no scan point crosses the fitted floor by more than log10(0.8); pub-
lished operating points are re-anchored to their own baseline at
matched capacity ratio with a 5% (0.22 dB) tolerance; and a du-
ality certificate lower-bounds the achieved error at every (budget,
view).
On a synthetic cartoon control with the same protocol, the
chord lands directly on Zador’s law (−0.549/ −0.548/ −0.551,
R2=0.997). On the 13 public scenes (seed 0; 3 seeds on gar-
den/room/bonsai) chord slopes are seed-stable (±0.004–0.011) but
all fall outside the preregistered band [−0.55,−0.45], a registered
negative. The reading-window audit explains it: the SfM point
clouds have TWO-NN intrinsic dimension 1.9–2.05, and the steep-
est three-band windows reach −0.41 to −0.53 across scenes and
seeds (bonsai −0.46/ −0.49/ −0.53; room −0.469 →−0.496
when the fine-tune budget is quadrupled), straddling the predicted
−1/2. Floor intactness holds 13/13; the duality certificate holds at
every (band, view) on room with tightness 0.28 →0.03 (Table S10,
full data in supplementary material).
The published frontier is flat: every compression method with
a same-paper (N,PSNR) pair defines a resolution-invariant two-
point trajectory (Fig. S3). Across Compact3DGS and EAGLES on
all 13 scenes plus Mini-Splatting [FW24] and RadSplat [NMR∗25]
at dataset level, these slopes occupy [−0.28,+0.09] with mean
−0.04: removing 60–70% of a converged model’s primitives costs
under 1 dB across the surveyed compressors, while the floor law
(asymptote −1/2, reached end-to-end on the synthetic control
and bracketed by our steepest windows) is an order of magnitude
steeper. Published compression operates deep in the over-budgeted
regime: its reported gains ride the redundancy θwaste = Nopt/Nfull
that the floor makes measurable, not proximity to capacity. The uni-
versal (model-free) colour-quantization floor, a lower bound on ev-
ery representation at budget N computed from test images alone,
confirms it: all 26 published operating points lie above the band
(Fig. S4).
Three questions therefore have checkable answers: certified
compression headroom (measure the floor once, place any candi-
date point on it; with our measured estimator looseness folded in,
headroom is certified on eight published points, up to +1.9 dB);
when lossless is impossible (the gap decomposition separates struc-
tural coupling loss from source capacity); and the right budget: the
floor knee N∗is the budget where the steepest-window slope is
largest, which is exactly where BCT trains (Section 5.1).
6.2. Training-side results
The redundancy signal and the stopping rule behave as the floor

The contribution-scored export of the score export of Section 5

ing beats opacity-ordered pruning, the baseline the same protocol
ships, by +2.7 to +7.9 dB (PSNR), with large margins also in

and −0.54 dB on room and bonsai versus the full model. At 25%
the loss is substantial (3.1–13.9 dB), which is the expected behavior
on already-compressed models: these trained models are down to
36–66% of Vanilla, so they carry less removable redundancy than
Block_A (where 24% of primitives are invisible from every training
view and the curve is flat to 25%). The clean division of labor: the
score export recovers what a post-hoc pass can, and the training-
side BCT is the tool where a fixed quality target must be met.
The two stages also compose. On room, we build factoring trees
over two roots, the full model (1.66M primitives) and the budget-
centered model trained at the floor’s knee (529k), and deploy both
through the same scheduler at a matched budget of ≈520k ren-
dered primitives (34 held-out views, LOD render protocol). The
BCT-born tree renders 34.19 dB, within 0.16 dB of its own root;
the full-born tree, collapsed to the same budget, renders 22.83 dB.
Budget-centered training is thus the correct front end for factoring-
tree deployment: a model born at the floor’s budget passes through
the tree essentially unchanged, because it contains almost no re-
dundancy for the tree to lose (per-tier data in the supplementary
material).

On garden (Table 2), the residual-proxy adaptive selector recovers

(1.05×).

comparisons: Fig. S12 for the garden tiers, Fig. S10 for the same

The two sides of the pipeline compose at city scale on our v3 retrain

MatrixCity Aerial model (8.56M primitives, full SH) renders at

1080p on one RTX 4080 SUPER (c=4/8/16: 1288/824/407 MB
on 8/4/2 GB-class targets); quality is visually preserved down to
the 2 GB tier (|∆PSNR| ≤0.27 dB vs. full on this held-out view),
with no retraining or re-export (Fig. 5).
We verify that the budget semantics carry to an interactive run-
time, and we measure where the real-time boundary actually lies
on our v3 retrain of the official Block_all data (6.89M primitives,
full SH, 25.43 dB on the official 207-view protocol with the 3DGS
rasterizer; Table 4). The deployment layer is CP-LOD (coverage-
preserving prefix LOD): per frame a leaf-only cut of the factree
is traversed on GPU, a capacity budget K bounds the material-
ized working set, Morton-strided subsampling with scale×√r and
opacity 1−(1−α)r compensation preserves coverage at any budget
(Table 4, Fig. 6b). Selection and gather run entirely on GPU—the
selected prefix is collected straight into the rasterizer buffers with
no CPU-side collection or host-to-device upload—and the GPU
selection reproduces the offline reference exactly, so the runtime
inherits the evaluation semantics measured above; on the official
207-view trajectory the per-frame selected count changes by a me-
dian of ∼1% between consecutive views, and residual popping is
confined to tier boundaries (Limitations). Three properties matter
for deployment. First, selection is resolution-invariant by construc-
tion: the materialized set is identical at 8002 and 1920 × 1080 at
every budget (measured equal counts on both); the naive threshold-
schedule drift of the tier prototype is gone. Second, the full set ren-
ders in real time: at native 1920 × 1080 with full view-dependent
SH on one RTX 4080 SUPER, the official 207-view test trajec-
tory renders at 71 fps mean (full set, full SH, full coverage; same
protocol as the 25.43 dB measurement; timing drops the first 10
frames, and the per-level fps curve is Table S8); the orbit probe
renders the full 5.99M at 49.5 fps; at 8002 the same full set runs
79–198 fps. As a commodity-8 GB reference, the DC-only com-
pact path renders the original 19.93M Block_all model at 30.7 fps
at 8002 on an RTX 3060 Ti (supplementary material). Third, the
capacity ladder is smooth: at 1080p, 0.34M/0.68M/1.38M/2.59M
selected render 76.8/65.0/59.0/57.0 fps at 18.1/20.3/21.7/23.4 dB
self-PSNR against the full render, so the budget–quality trade-off
is continuous and deploy-time (Fig. S14b).

6.7. Ablations
On the training side (lego), replacing the redundancy-localizing
θ map by a random region prior costs 11–13% more primitives
at the same PSNR; the θ signal’s win is compression, not accu-
racy. Without the rate-based stopping criterion, the default densifi-
cation controller over-prunes and loses 0.56 dB; with it, training
freezes automatically at the sweet spot (step 5000), and a stop-
only variant (no θ pruning) still reaches 37.00 dB at 216k (63%
of vanilla, −0.19 dB), isolating the stopping rule as the accuracy-
critical component. Hyper-parameter sensitivity is ≤0.10 dB. On
the rendering side, a DC-only tree loses 5 dB systematically (stor-
ing opacity-weighted SH first moments for all bands is required),
and the residual-proxy selection recovers +0.49 dB over the fixed
threshold at a comparable count (garden, Table 2).
6.8. Efficiency
Construction also fits the desktop GPU for the Block_all trees re-
ported here: on the 10M θ-pruned Block_all tree (full SH moments)
it peaks at 6.45 GiB on the 8 GB RTX 3060 Ti (2.53 GB output);
the 7.07M Block_all v2 tree (full SH, 1.87 GB) builds on the same
consumer card. (The MatrixCity Aerial tree is a one-time offline
build.)
7. Limitations
We evaluate on a single GPU; transparent and occluded-region
extremes are not covered, and memory streaming (GPS’s native
regime) remains future work. The proved layer covers the com-
positing renderer family, atomic and field representations, and
the consistency functional with matching upper and sharp lower
bounds; three items remain open—the sharpness of the visibility-
exponent bound, SGD trainability of implicit fields at the proved
N−1/2 rate, and non-compositing renderers such as caustics and
participating media. The measured −1/2 bracket holds on all 13
scenes inside the registered reading window (indoor scenes read

Records behind Section 6.1 of the main paper: the preregistered

S3.1. Protocol and records
The floor is only useful if it can be verified on real scenes. The floor-
measurement study is preregistered (criteria frozen before data col-
lection) and its verdicts were frozen before reading the data, a dis-
cipline mirrored by our other measurements (Appendix S5).
S3.2. Protocol
On each scene: (i) scan budgets (MCMC training at cap 2 × 106
primitives, then opacity-prune to N and fine-tune) to get the empir-
ical floor curve R(N) (9 budgets); (ii) floor intactness: no scan point
crosses the fitted floor by more than log10(0.8); (iii) published oper-
ating points are placed against the floor, re-anchored to the paper’s
own baseline at matched capacity ratio, 5% tolerance ≈0.22 dB;
(iv) duality certificates lower-bound achieved error at every (bud-
get, view).
S4. Background and guarantees
We collect the formal background behind the design principles of
Section 4.4 of the main paper: planks and weighted covering, the
adaptive approximation rate, the optimal budget allocation, and the
rendering-side error control. Proofs of the weighted-covering the-
orem, the adaptive-covering-rate theorem and its converse, and the
derivation of the budget-allocation corollary are given here; the
rendering-side error-control statements are summarized in the sub-
section below.
S4.1. From primitives to planks
A trained model is a set of planks (µi,Ri,hi,αi): each primitive
occupies a box of half-extents hi = 3σi oriented by Ri, carrying
weight αi. Dropping primitives means replacing one covering by
a coarser one; the two quantities that decide whether this is safe
are local covering density (is this region over-covered, i.e. are its
planks redundant?) and approximation error (how much mass is
lost per removed plank?). The theorems below answer exactly these
two questions, which is why the rendering and training sides of
Sections 6 and 7 of the main paper both reduce to them.
S4.2. Weighted covering
Definition 3 (Plank). A plank V is a box in R3 carrying a weight
wV : V →[0,1] (effective opacity contribution); |V| is its volume.
A 3DGS primitive is a plank: center µ, principal axes from its rota-
tion, half-extents h = 3σ (three-sigma convention), weight = opac-
ity.

S4.5. Rendering-side error control
Replacing primitives by equivalent planks changes the rendered im-
age; the end-to-end certificate bounds this change in the audited
domain: per-view image error is controlled by the field error trans-
ferred through the rendering operator term plus a model-operator
gap classified by a saturation-front decomposition. In practice we
do not rely on the (loose) worst-case constants: the tree addition-
ally stores per-cluster residual statistics, geometric spread σgeo and
SH-residual ratio ρsh, which act as an empirical error proxy for the
switch decision (Section 5.3 of the main paper). We verify on real
scenes that the certificate remains valid (never violated in measure-
ments) but loose; this is stated as a limitation (Section 7 of the main
paper).

S8. Tree hyper-parameter sensitivity
The factoring tree’s two defaults (aggregate cap 256, leaf capac-

S9. Timing of BCT vs. the traditional route
Measured on room@529k (one A100, neighbor jobs present on

78±7 min over three seeds. The traditional route for the same bud-

S10. Scheduler enhancements and export
This section collects the deployment-side enhancements of the
pixel-footprint scheduler (Section 5.3 of the main paper): adaptive

Engineering notes (moved from the main text). Construction
of the tree on 8.56M primitives takes 1.5 s with a 19.4 GiB peak on
an A100 GPU. The compact binary stores raw primitives in Mor-
ton order, per-band equivalent planks, and SH first moments of all
degrees; per frame, the coverage-preserving prefix cut is traversed
on GPU and the selected prefix is gathered by a per-primitive ker-
nel directly into the rasterizer buffers (fused with SH evaluation
and opacity compensation), with no CPU-side collection or host-
to-device upload: 0.8 ms preprocess including selection+gather on
the 7.07M tree at 1920 × 1080. The GPU selection reproduces the
offline Python reference exactly (identical per-view tier counts on
5/5 views at c=1 and 10/10 at c=8). The DC-only compact path
stores 3 floats per primitive and selects 13.1M primitives at 3.9 GB
on 8 GB GPUs. On the official 207-view trajectory at capacity
K=2M the per-frame selected count changes by a median of 1.1%
between consecutive views; the large-count outliers coincide with
view-direction reversals, where the leaves entering or leaving the
selection are back-facing for the new view and are never rasterized.
The serial experiment of Section 6.2 of the main paper (room, 34
held-out views, LOD render protocol) is summarized below; per-
view data and the script are archived with the paper artifacts.
For adaptive (scene-content-aware) selection we additionally
store per cluster two residuals: the geometric spread σgeo =
p
tr(T ∗)/W (world units, projected to pixels by σ · f/d) and the
SH residual ratio ρsh (cluster-internal SH variance vs. global RMS).
These proxy the switch error analyzed in the error-control sec-

+0.49 dB at a comparable primitive count (1.05×; Table 2 of the

The scheduler emits a standard primitive stream (means, quater-
nions, scales, opacities, SH), consumable by any splatting raster-
izer (gsplat, CityGaussian, etc.). For cross-renderer evaluation we
export each tier selection to standard 3DGS PLY files (SH zero-

them with the GPS renderer [RPWM26] (Section 5.3 of the main
paper).
A purely geometric redundancy signal has a blind spot: a prim-
itive can be geometrically redundant yet visually load-bearing (it
occludes its neighbors, or carries a colour no neighbor repro-
duces), or geometrically isolated yet invisible (fully occluded in
every training view). Aggressive pruning exposes exactly this gap:
a geometry-only score removes primitives that a contribution-aware
score would keep (Table S7); the underlying occlusion term is an
open item in the error control of the supplementary material.
For pure primitive-count budgets, the same export path sup-
ports a render-aware ranking. We score every primitive with the
LightGaussian significance [FWW∗24] (the accumulated per-pixel
Tα over all training views, where T is the front-to-back trans-
mittance) via a one-pass forward scan over the training cameras
(234 s for 4565 views at 7.94M primitives on an RTX 4080 SU-
PER). Our CUDA accumulation replicates the reference rasterizer’s
per-pixel semantics exactly (validated by the telescoping identity
∑i si = ∑p(1−Tfinal), matched to 10−5 relative on random scenes).
Any budget point is then a top-N re-export of the same trained
model. On Block_A, 24.3% of the trained primitives score exactly
zero, never visible from any training view, and the resulting budget
axis stays at Vanilla quality down to 25% of the primitives (Sec-
tion 6.5 of the main paper).
