# What Builds the Scene? Luminance Dominates Geometry Formation in 3D Gaussian Splatting 

Rezvan Joshaghani Steven Cutchin

RezvanJoshaghani@u.boisestate.edu StevenCutchin@boisestate.edu 

# Abstract 

Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) learns geometry and appearance jointly from RGB supervision, making it difficult to isolate how luminance and chroma contribute to the learned representation. We study this by training models under different channel supervision, freezing their non-appearance parameters (position, scale, rotation, and opacity), and re-estimating appearance with the same solver before comparing held-out reconstruction. Across eleven benchmark scenes with four independent runs each, geometry learned from luminance alone supports held-out reconstruction 0.085 dB below RGB-trained geometry on average. If chroma is deleted from a trained model, a sufficiently expressive solver can re-fit it on the frozen geometry to the original quality or slightly better. Higher-order spherical harmonics contribute much more reconstruction quality to luminance than to chroma, improving PSNR by 1.44 dB versus 0.19 dB on average, although on mirror-like surfaces hue does still change with viewpoint. The luminance advantage is even larger when geometry is being formed. Chroma-only supervision produces geometry 3.9–5.5 dB worse than luminance-only supervision after the same appearance solve; densification explains part of this gap. Overall, geometry formation in standard 3DGS is strongly luminance-dominated but not luminance-exclusive, and much of the chromatic appearance can be recovered after spatial support has formed. 

# 1 Introduction 

Standard RGB optimization in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [9] combines two roles of the image signal: building the scene and coloring the scene . A standard degree-3 model represents appearance with 16 spherical-harmonic (SH) coefficients per color channel, or 48 appearance coefficients per Gaussian. Because SH evaluation is linear in its coefficients, these appearance parameters can be re-expressed under a linear change of color basis. For example, YCbCr separates the image signal into one luminance-like channel, Y , and two chromatic channels, Cb and Cr . Yet these appearance parameters are not learned independently of scene structure. Standard 3DGS jointly optimizes Gaussian positions, scales, rotations, opacities, and SH coefficients against the same image-space reconstruction loss, while densification and pruning are driven by statistics of that optimization. An RGB residual therefore does not reveal which part of the color signal caused the representation to form. A simple training intervention exposes this ambiguity. We withhold chromatic supervision during the first half of training and optimize using only Y ; Cb and Cr supervision are introduced later (Fig. 1b). When chroma is released, held-out reconstruction quality rises sharply, yet the delayed schedules often converge near ordinary RGB training. This does not tell us why. Luminance may already have built the geometry, with chroma filling in appearance; alternatively, the later full-color phase may repair geometry left incomplete by luminance. A successful final reconstruction after delayed chroma therefore does not tell us which signal actually built the representation. 

Preprint. arXiv:2610.00749v1 [cs.CV] 30 Sep 2026 Several results already point to a separation of these roles along the luminance–chroma axis. Monochromatic reconstruction followed by colorization shows that a useful spatial representa-tion can be learned without observed chroma and colored afterward [3, 5, 29]. Instant Colorization shows that appearance can be re-estimated on a fixed Gaussian representation without further geo-metric optimization [12]. LCD-GS and palette-based editing separate luminance-like and chromatic components explicitly, for HDR reconstruction and for appearance manipulation [34, 2]. GSICO, HiGS, and YUV-domain channel decorrelation show that higher-order chromatic SH can be pruned or coarsely quantized at little cost [15, 19, 26]. Outside neural rendering, image coding has long subsampled chroma and geometric vision runs on intensity alone [28, 13, 24]. Together these results establish feasibility: structure can exist without observed chroma, color can be fitted after geometry exists, and chromatic appearance can be stored compactly. Natural-image statistics supply a prior for what to expect. In natural images, luminance carries most of the spatial detail and contrast, while the chromatic channels are smoother and lower in amplitude [23, 16]. That prior predicts that luminance should matter more for geometry. It does not tell us how much worse chroma-only training will perform, or how much of that gap comes from densification, the process that adds Gaussians during training. We test whether the gap persists when both conditions keep the same set of Gaussians. We also measure how much chroma can be fitted on fixed geometry and how much each channel group benefits from view-dependent SH terms. These prior methods do not establish what chromatic supervision contributes to geometry learned during RGB training. Nor do they measure how changing when observed chroma enters the loss affects the learned geometry. The Delayed-Chroma result shows that final reconstruction quality alone cannot distinguish the possible explanations. To separate them, we freeze Gaussian position, scale, rotation, and opacity after training and apply the same full degree-3 appearance solve A∗

to each geometry (Fig. 1a; Sec. 4). This measures the reconstruction each geometry can support after appearance is re-estimated under the same conditions. Throughout, geometry means the non-appearance parameters of the model, including opacity, and every model starts from the SfM initialization provided with its dataset. Our claims therefore concern reconstruction of the captured benchmark images after standard 3DGS optimization and densification from that initialization; they do not concern the physical accuracy of its surfaces, and they do not concern geometry formed without a spatial prior. We combine this comparison with fixed-geometry chroma recovery and the SH-capacity sweep (Sec. 5), and with direct changes to channel supervision (Sec. 6). All experiments use BT.601 YCbCr and the eleven-scene 3DGS benchmark unless stated otherwise.

Part of this gap comes from densification, because chroma produces smaller positional gradients and therefore fewer new Gaussians. This effect is consistent but much smaller than the effect of removing luminance. 

3. Higher-order SH matters far more for luminance than for chroma. Removing SH degrees 1–3 lowers held-out PSNR by 1.441 dB for luminance but only 0.187 dB for chroma on average, and luminance loses more on every benchmark scene. In linear terms, held-out MSE rises by 35% for luminance and 3.6% for chroma. The same ordering holds on specular scenes. This does not mean 2that chroma is view independent. On the chrome ball, measured directly from the ground-truth views, chromaticity changes substantially with viewpoint. 

4. Chroma can be fully re-estimated on fixed geometry. We remove the chroma from trained RGB models and re-estimate it from the training images with the geometry frozen. Recovery rises from 0.300 with a single scene-wide tint to 1.017 with coupled degree-3 fitting, where 1 matches the trained model. Most of the chroma loss seen with simpler solves therefore comes from the solver, not from the geometry. Because this solve recovers appearance fully, we use it to compare geometries trained under different supervision without carrying over the appearance each model learned. 

5. Delayed chroma follows the same asymmetry. Withholding chroma for the first half of training preserves final quality on two of three replicated scenes and costs 0.27 dB on BONSAI . When chroma is introduced, quality rises sharply, and the rise moves with the release time rather than with other training events such as the end of densification. When training continues from the same luminance-only checkpoint, adding chroma improves the reconstruction the final geometry supports by about 0.1 dB, which matches the RGB advantage measured in the main comparison. The pattern largely holds in an independent 3DGS implementation and in BT.709, YCoCg, and YIQ. Together, these results show that luminance provides most of the signal for learning geometry that supports reconstruction, and most of the benefit of view-dependent appearance. Chroma plays a smaller role in forming geometry, and most of its appearance can be fitted afterward with little higher-order SH. However, the asymmetry is not absolute. Chroma can still change the learned geometry, RGB-trained geometry keeps an advantage on three scenes, and view-dependent chromatic reflections can require higher-order chromatic SH. 

# 2 Related Work 

The distinction between luminance and chroma has appeared in several areas of 3D reconstruction and Gaussian Splatting. It has been used for monochromatic reconstruction and colorization, appearance editing, progressive training, and compression. These works provide useful evidence about what can be learned from luminance alone and how chromatic appearance can be represented after training. They do not directly measure what chroma contributes while standard RGB-supervised 3DGS learns its geometry. 

2.1 Monochromatic Reconstruction and 3D Colorization 

Several methods reconstruct scenes from monochromatic views and add color afterward. Cheng et al. [3] learn luminance and density from monochromatic multiview images, then predict color for the reconstructed radiance field. ChromaDistill [5] studies a similar problem primarily with Plenoxels and also reports a 3DGS variant in its supplementary material. In that variant, the model is trained with luminance alone, and chroma is introduced through knowledge distillation from an image colorization model. Color3D [29] extends this to both static and dynamic scenes. It combines an image colorizer with a Lab-space Gaussian representation in which luminance and chroma are optimized separately. In all three cases, the input images contain no chromatic information. Color is estimated afterward using an image colorization model. Our setting removes chroma at a different point. Monochromatic reconstruction starts from input images with no chromatic observations. We start from the same RGB images used in standard 3DGS training. One model is trained on luminance alone and the other receives full RGB supervision. We then compare the learned geometries after the same full-appearance solve. This measures what chromatic supervision contributes while geometry is being learned.

Luminance-GS [4] addresses difficult illumination through per-view color mapping and view-adaptive curve correction during 3DGS training. Thinking Like Van Gogh [36] is a 3D stylizer that separates luminance-related structure from color to control the geometric deformation separately from appear-ance changes. 3RGB images

2.3 Appearance Recovery on Fixed Geometry 

Other work solves for appearance after the Gaussian geometry has already been learned. Instant Colorization [12] is the closest algorithmic precedent for our post-training appearance recovery experiments. Lieber et al. [12] freeze a pretrained Gaussian representation and solve for new appearance coefficients using visibility-weighted least squares. Their formulation supports view-dependent spherical-harmonic appearance and applications such as colorization, relighting, and attribute transfer. Zhou [37] uses a similar setup to determine which spherical-harmonic coefficients can be recovered from the training views and remove poorly constrained directions from the solve. Chao and Gingold [2] keep geometry fixed while reparameterizing appearance for palette-based color and luminance editing. A good reconstruction after an appearance solve does not guarantee the physical correctness of the underlying geometry. Wang and Miller [30] show that view-dependent appearance can compensate for geometric errors while the reconstructed images still look correct. We therefore use reconstruction 4after appearance recovery to measure what a learned geometry can support, not whether it matches the true surface. We build on Instant Colorization and extend its formulation to arbitrary subsets of color channels in an affine color space. Solver quality matters because incomplete appearance recovery can reduce reconstruction quality even when the geometry supports a better solution. We therefore test several solvers and use the one that best reconstructs the training images from fixed geometry. The formulation and solver evaluation are described in Sec. 3. 

2.4 Progressive Supervision in 3DGS Training 

Several methods control when different parts of the training signal become available. FreGS [33] applies coarse-to-fine frequency regularization to encourage stable geometry before finer detail is introduced. Opti3DGS [6] progressively reduces blur in the training images so that high-frequency spatial detail enters later in training. AutoOpti3DGS [17] learns this schedule with a wavelet transform rather than setting it in advance. PDF-GS [25] filters distracting content across training phases before the final reconstruction. These methods show that the timing of supervision can affect the geometry learned during training. Opti3DGS highlights this connection by linking delayed fine-scale supervision to reduced premature densification. Our Delayed-Chroma experiment applies this idea to color. We withhold chroma early in training and introduce it later, while luminance remains available from the start. The 3DGS variant of ChromaDistill [5] uses the same luminance-first schedule, training on luminance and adding chroma afterward. The difference here is that our chroma is observed in the training images rather than distilled from a colorization model, so the schedule tests what observed chroma adds. The experiment is not intended as a new training strategy. We use it to test whether the learned geometry changes when chromatic supervision becomes available. 

2.5 Appearance Compression in Trained 3DGS Models 

Prior work also examines how much appearance capacity a trained 3DGS model needs. Methods reduce storage through compact or quantized Gaussian representations [18, 11], adaptive SH allo-cation and memory-aware parameter reduction [20], and entropy coding of Gaussian attributes [8]. Much of the stored appearance capacity can be removed or represented more compactly without substantially reducing reconstruction quality. These methods focus on compression rather than why the redundancy differs between luminance and chroma or how that difference arises during training. GSICO [15], HiGS [19], and Sony’s YUV-domain channel-decorrelation method [26] provide more direct evidence of this difference. GSICO transforms SH coefficients from RGB to BT.601 YUV and removes chrominance AC components before image-based coding. Sony’s method transforms the SH coefficients into YUV and retains the base color and luminance terms while removing higher-order chromatic coefficients. HiGS uses quantization rather than pruning. Its optional packed SH representation converts the coefficients to YCoCg-R and assigns 6 bits to luminance and 4 bits to each chromatic component, reducing per-Gaussian SH storage from 192 to 32 bytes while maintaining nearly the same rendering quality. These results show that chromatic SH can tolerate more aggressive compression than luminance. We investigate whether this post-training difference is related to the different roles played by luminance and chroma during geometry learning.

3.2 Color space 

To separate luminance from chroma, we transform the training images, background color, and SH appearance coefficients from RGB to full-range BT.601 YCbCr: 

Y = 0 .299 R + 0 .587 G + 0 .114 B, (1) 

Cb = ( B − Y )/1.772 + 0 .5, (2) 

Cr = ( R − Y )/1.402 + 0 .5. (3) Here Y is luma computed from the encoded RGB images, not physical luminance; we retain the term “luminance” for consistency. The affine offset is applied only to the SH DC term. Higher-order coefficients receive the linear part of the transform because they represent directional changes around the base color. The implementation removes the rasterizer’s +0 .5 color bias from the DC term, applies the affine transform, and restores the bias. For full-range BT.601 the offsets cancel exactly, because the Y row of the matrix sums to one and the Cb and Cr rows sum to zero, so the net DC transform is the linear matrix alone; the two paths agree to 10 −15 numerically. The rasterizer applies a color clamp, so rendering in RGB and then converting the image to YCbCr is not exactly identical to rendering directly in YCbCr. In our validation, the difference is below 0.11 dB. All fixed-geometry comparisons use the same working color space, rendering path, appearance solve, and inverse transform. This standardizes evaluation, but does not rule out geometry-dependent effects of clamping. 

3.3 Training conditions 

Standard RGB training uses the usual L1+D-SSIM loss function over all three RGB channels. The 

Y condition uses the same loss function on the luminance channel only, while the Cb and Cr 

coefficients receive zero gradient and remain unsupervised. Because the L1 term is averaged over the supervised channels, supervising one channel does not change the loss scale by a factor of three. Unsupervised coefficients stay at their SfM initialization and are copied when Gaussians are cloned or split. The common solve replaces these coefficients before evaluation, but does not undo any effects they may have had during training. Delayed Chroma also begins with luminance-only supervision, but Cb and Cr are introduced at a chosen release iteration. The complementary CbCr condition supervises only the two chromatic channels. We call each training condition an arm. In this paper, geometry is denoted by G and includes Gaussian position, scale, rotation, and opacity. Opacity is included because 3DGS treats it as a spatial-support parameter: it drives pruning and determines 6which primitives contribute to a pixel. Opacity also affects rendered color, so it can retain appearance information that the SH solve does not reset; see the limitations. The SH appearance coefficients are not included in G.The RGB and Y objectives differ in color basis as well as in channel content, because neither L1

nor SSIM is invariant to the YCbCr transform. Two controls bound this confound. A basis control trains the YCbCr SH parameterization under the stock RGB loss, so that only the appearance basis differs from RGB training. A full-YCbCr loss arm, which averages the stock loss over the three transformed channels, changes the scale and structure of the image-space gradients. This changes the densification operating point: how readily the gradients cross the fixed threshold and trigger new Gaussians. We report this arm only as a diagnostic. A reduced-luminance control tests whether weaker gradients explain the lower Gaussian count. It multiplies the whole luminance loss, including both L1 and D-SSIM, by a fixed per-scene factor 1/s .Here s is the ratio of the luminance-only to the chroma-only densification statistic, frozen before this arm’s outcome was inspected; pilot results from earlier arms were already available. 3.6 Controls and interventions 

We use additional controls to check whether differences attributed to supervision are instead caused by changes in the number of Gaussians or in the densification process. To control for representation size, if a luminance-trained model contains more Gaussians than its RGB counterpart, we remove the lowest-opacity Gaussians until both models contain the same number and then rerun A∗. This opacity-pruning diagnostic replaced the retraining at matched count that was originally planned (supplement). It tests whether the luminance result depends simply on having more primitives. We also compare rendered expected depth between the RGB- and luminance-trained models. Because independently trained 3DGS models vary even under the same condition, we compare these depth differences with the variation observed between repeated runs of the same training condition. The full depth analysis is reported in the supplement. To test whether luminance itself is more effective than chroma at forming useful geometry, we also train models using chroma alone. We compare A∗(GCbCr ) with A∗(GY ) on GARDEN , COUNTER ,and KITCHEN . Chroma-only training produces smaller values of the positional-gradient statistic used by 3DGS to trigger densification. It can therefore produce fewer new Gaussians even if the training schedule is otherwise unchanged. To separate this densification effect from the information carried by chroma, we run an additional chroma condition with a lower densification threshold. The threshold is chosen so that the fraction of Gaussians crossing it at the first densification event matches the luminance condition; all other settings remain unchanged. A densification-free comparison removes the confound outright: both arms start from the same dense point set, taken from a stock RGB run at 15k iterations, and train with densification, pruning, and opacity resets disabled, so that both arms keep the same set of primitives, with the same count and identities, while their positions, shapes, and opacities evolve separately. A contrast-stretched chroma condition scales Cb and Cr to the standard deviation of luminance inside the loss, with the densification threshold re-matched on the stretched statistic, to test whether the D-SSIM term’s response to low-contrast chroma explains part of the residual. Finally, we test whether incorrect chroma can disturb geometry when luminance remains correct. In this condition, luminance is preserved to floating-point precision before quantization, and the recorded uint8 conversion changes it by at most 0.5/255 , while each training view receives another view’s chroma through a derangement of the optimization views, in which no view keeps its own chroma and no test or validation view contributes any. Each seed uses its own derangement, generated from its training seed. The luminance structure is therefore unchanged up to rounding, while the chromatic information is deliberately inconsistent across views. This experiment tests whether chroma can substantially alter geometry even when the luminance signal remains available. It is run with four seeds per arm on every scene, against the four-run RGB and luminance-only references of the main comparison. To check the effect of Gaussian count, we prune the lowest-opacity Gaussians from the RGB reference to match the adversarial count whenever the counts differ by more than 5%.

# 7 Delayed Chroma Is Consistent with the Measured Asymmetry 

The delayed-chroma observation that motivated the study becomes easier to interpret after the geometry and appearance tests. On replicated runs, withholding chroma for the first half of training largely preserves final quality on GARDEN (+0 .084 dB, SD 0.081 ) and STUMP (+0 .029 dB, SD 

0.148 ), while BONSAI retains a −0.268 dB deficit (SD 0.073 ). These SDs describe the paired differences over three runs per arm. Thus the robust result is preservation on some scenes, not a general improvement over RGB training. When chroma is released, held-out PSNR rises by about 1.8 dB over the next 2k iterations. Moving release from 10k to 20k moves the jump with it, whereas 2,000-iteration windows around the end of densification show changes of only 0.10 –0.13 dB. The release jump is expected because previously unsupervised channels begin to improve, so it is not itself evidence for geometry equivalence. Chroma can be fitted rapidly even while geometry and luminance appearance continue to change. Freezing both at the release and training only chromatic appearance to 30k costs

# 8 Discussion 

The experiments identify two distinct luminance–chroma asymmetries in trained 3DGS. Along the geometry axis, luminance-only supervision usually forms a non-appearance representation that supports nearly the same reconstruction as RGB after SH appearance is re-estimated identically, whereas chroma-only supervision forms a substantially weaker representation. Along the appearance axis, higher-order SH contributes far more reconstruction quality to luminance than to chroma. These axes are related but should not be collapsed into the claim that luminance is “geometry” and chroma is “color.” RGB retains reproducible scene-specific geometry advantages, conflicting chroma can perturb optimization, and view-dependent chromatic reflection can require higher-order SH. With four runs per condition on every scene, the eleven-scene gap is −0.085 ± 0.037 dB. At seed 0, SSIM moves with it by only 0.001 and LPIPS favors luminance-trained geometry on average; the gap does not depend on the appearance basis in the mean, and extending the solver budget changes the gap little on the two tested scenes. The fixed-geometry recovery experiments are important because they change what a failed colorization result means. An independent per-Gaussian fit initially leaves a visible chromatic deficit, but most of that deficit disappears once residuals are attributed jointly across overlapping Gaussians. Aweak inverse operator can therefore make a fixed spatial representation look less capable than it is. Conversely, recovery above the trained checkpoint does not imply hidden chroma in geometry; it reflects a quadratic post-hoc appearance objective, closer to the PSNR metric than the training loss, and access to the target training images, including views that training withheld. The densification intervention shows that part of the geometry asymmetry is algorithmic. Chroma produces weaker values of the stock absolute densification statistic, so it receives fewer primitives under the default threshold. Correcting that gate at the first event recovers about 40% of the loss. Densification driven by a fixed gradient threshold is known to be sensitive to gradient magnitude [22, 32, 14]; our result shows that the channel content of the loss moves that operating point. Starting both arms from the same RGB-placed dense set with densification off recovers a further 0.6–1.1 dB, a step that changes the initialization and the densifier together, and leaves 1.4–2.5 dB with identical primitives. That set was placed under RGB supervision, so this remainder holds under a shared RGB-placed initialization; it is a property of the chromatic signal under the tested loss and optimizer rather than of the densifier. Separately, equalizing chromatic contrast in the loss recovers at most 9% 

of the threshold-matched residual; the two controls were not combined. The channel hierarchy is therefore a property of image statistics interacting with the 3DGS objective, not of the densifier alone. These findings suggest three possible changes to 3DGS, which we have not evaluated. Geometry-oriented operations such as densification could use luminance-weighted signals; chromatic appearance could be fitted or replaced after spatial support is established; and chromatic SH degree or precision could be allocated more aggressively than luminance. These observations are consistent with existing monochromatic reconstruction [3, 5, 29], fixed-geometry colorization [12], and YUV-domain compression methods [15, 19, 26]. Our experiments measure the conditions that make this division of labor possible and show that the asymmetry also appears during training. This motivates training procedures that treat luminance and chroma differently. First, luminance is not universally sufficient: RGB-trained geometry keeps replicated advantages on KITCHEN , DR . JOHNSON , and PLAYROOM , and where a boundary is carried only by chroma, luminance-only training blurs its depth. Second, chroma is not geometrically irrelevant: conflicting chroma costs 0.15 –0.67 dB of geometry-supported reconstruction, replicated across seeds that each carry their own donor mapping. Third, chromatic appearance is not inherently view independent; on 17 the chrome ball its relative view dependence matches that of luminance, even though its higher-order share of the recoverable span is smaller. Fourth, the RGB-versus-Y comparison changes the color basis of the training loss together with the channel content; the basis control bounds this in the mean, but a loss-basis change cannot be removed without moving the densification operating point. Fifth, the causal channel study covers three scenes, with four seeds for the adversarial arm (each with its own donor mapping) and one seed for the chroma-only, threshold-matched, densification-free, contrast-stretched, and fork arms, and the opponent-basis sweep uses one seed per cell. Sixth, the direct visible-geometry analysis is relative to retraining variation rather than ground-truth surfaces. In the object region of an analytic room with exact depth, luminance-only depth error matches RGB within 2% on diffuse and colored-specular materials, but is much larger where luminance is flat. The prespecified criterion uses the whole image, where grazing-angle walls dominate and RGB itself fails the criterion. The object-region results are therefore descriptive. Testing a criterion fixed in advance for that region, with fresh seeds, is left to future work. Seventh, all conclusions remain specific to the tested 3DGS regimes; sparse-view settings, NeRFs, and other primitive families are natural next tests. Eighth, capture and image processing can alter chroma before training. Demosaicing and in-camera processing can smooth chromatic detail. The inspected benchmark JPEGs use 4:2:0 chroma subsam-pling, which stores chroma at half the luminance resolution in each dimension. Their near-lossless quantization tables do not establish that subsampling was the only loss of chromatic detail, or reveal earlier processing and compression. Training loads the Mip-NeRF 360 images downscaled by 1.95 –

3.24 × (Sec. 3), which brings the stored chroma grid close to the training grid but cannot restore detail already lost, whereas the Tanks and Temples and Deep Blending scenes train at native resolution, with chroma at half the training resolution; the three scenes of the channel study are Mip-NeRF 360 scenes. The chroma-only deficit is therefore a property of captured chroma under this pipeline; a controlled capture or render without chroma subsampling could reduce it, and we have not tested that. The conclusions concern the reconstruction a representation supports after standard optimization and densification from that initialization, and opacity can retain photometric structure that the appearance solve does not reset; re-solving opacity would require a nonlinear solve, which we do not attempt. 

# 9 Conclusion 

Standard RGB 3DGS training entangles the signals that build spatial support with the coefficients that color it. Separating those roles reveals a consistent but non-absolute asymmetry. Within standard SfM-initialized 3DGS, luminance provides the dominant signal for forming geometry that enables reconstruction and carries most of the benefit of higher-order directional appearance. Chroma contributes a weaker geometry signal, can measurably perturb optimization, and sometimes matters early, but much of its appearance can be re-estimated later on fixed support with low angular capacity. This division of labor explains why delayed chroma can often preserve final quality and suggests that future 3DGS systems need not allocate identical training and representation capacity to luminance and chroma. 18 A Supplementary Material

A.3 Protocol Amendments 

Three departures from the prespecified protocol were made before the results were finalized, and we record them here. First, the original rule excluded solves that reached the iteration cap without meeting the stopping tolerance. Every solve of the main RGB-versus-Y comparison reached the cap, in both arms alike, so the rule was amended to retain capped solves and the 200-iteration convergence check of Sec. A was added in its place. Second, the Gaussian-count control was planned as retraining at a matched count. It was replaced by the opacity-pruning diagnostic reported above, which removes the lowest-opacity Gaussians from the larger model and re-solves A∗; this is cheaper and holds the trained geometry fixed, but it does not test what training would have produced at the matched count. Third, the adversarial-chroma arm was first run with donor views drawn from all views of each scene, including benchmark test views and validation views, and with one mapping shared by all seeds. It was rerun with donors restricted to the optimization views and a fresh mapping per seed, and only the rerun is reported. The per-scene deficits moved by at most 0.10 dB between the two runs and every gate outcome was unchanged.

The isoluminant-boundary scene is a procedural plate in front of a backdrop, unlit, whose rim is a pure chroma step on its upper half and a chroma-plus-luminance step on its lower half as a within-scene control; luminance across the isoluminant rim stays within 0.11 /255 in every view. RGB, luminance-only, and chroma-only arms were trained with two seeds each. Under the prespecified criterion the difference-in-differences is +0 .028 against a 0.03 cutoff, so the failure is not confirmed on that rule; post hoc, luminance-only depth is pulled 19% –30% toward the backdrop across the isoluminant rim against 4.5% across the luminance rim, RGB and chroma-only stay near 2% , and chroma-only localizes the rim with about 79k Gaussians. The backdrop side of the rim is unidentifiable for every arm by construction. The ground-truth geometry suite is an analytic room rendered under three material cases (diffuse, colored-specular, and flat-luminance with isoluminant chroma patches), RGB and luminance-only with two seeds each; its prespecified gate, written on all pixels, is failed by RGB itself ( 5.6% –7.0% depth error against a 5% gate) because the room walls dominate at grazing incidence. On the objects region, post hoc, luminance-only depth error is 1.00 × and 1.02 × RGB’s on the diffuse and specular cases and 15 .6× on the chroma case, where the luminance-only model densifies to about a quarter of RGB’s primitives and reaches 22 .0 against 39 .9 dB after A∗.25 Acknowledgements
