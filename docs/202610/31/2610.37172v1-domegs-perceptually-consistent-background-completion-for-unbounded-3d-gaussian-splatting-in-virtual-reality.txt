ABSTRACT
The growing need for high-quality 3D content remains among the
most prevalent challenges in virtual reality (VR). Recently, novel
view synthesis techniques have enabled rapid content creation, with
the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) emerging as one of the most
promising methods for generating high-fidelity 3D scenes. How-
ever, using 3DGS to reconstruct unbounded outdoor scenes can
leave horizons incomplete due to missing data or a lack of dis-
tant geometric constraints. Such incompleteness can break immer-
sion, compromising VR’s core characteristic. A typical workaround
in traditional VR systems is a textured cube (skybox) or sphere
(skydome) surrounding the environment to create the illusion of a
distant background. However, these approaches struggle to blend
seamlessly with the reconstructed 3DGS geometry.
To address
these issues, we introduce the DomeGS framework 1, which incor-
porates the skydome concept not as a flat background shell, but as a
part of the reconstructed Gaussian Splatting 3D scene. By integrat-

INTRODUCTION
Realistic, highly immersive experiences are a core requirement in
spatial interfaces such as virtual reality (VR) [30, 9] and a pri-
mary objective within the spatial computing community [16, 42]. A
key factor shaping these experiences is the high-quality 3D content
that populates fully virtual environments [32]. Recently, 3D Gaus-
sian Splatting (3DGS) [20] has emerged as a promising method of
scene representation and rendering for interactive VR systems, pro-
viding high-fidelity 3D reconstruction while also enabling complex
physics-aware interactions [17, 26]. 3DGS is a real-time view syn-
thesis method that offers high visual quality at frame rates feasible
for head-mounted displays (HMDs).
3DGS works best when reconstructing near-field, bounded
scenes with compelling depth cues. However, when we attempt
to reconstruct unbounded outdoor environments, 3DGS reveals a
critical constraint caused by sparseness or entirely missing far-field
detail in the capture data (e.g. skies, distant facades, and the hori-
zon). Without sufficient multi-view parallax and geometric land-
marks, the 3DGS optimization process lacks the details needed to
accurately triangulate depth in these regions [1]. As a result, stan-
dard 3DGS models fail to reconstruct plausible geometry in the dis-
tance [27, 36]. In immersive VR, this failure can negatively affect
immersion, as users may encounter jagged boundaries, floating ar-
tifacts, or gaps in the virtual environment that break the illusion of
arXiv:2610.37172v1  [cs.GR]  27 Oct 2026


a continuous unbounded outdoor scene [38, 43] (see Fig. 1).
A common workaround to mask these missing regions, natively
supported in game engines such as Unity2, is to project a panoramic
image onto an infinitely distant skybox.
Here, recent advance-
ments have made significant strides in unbounded 3D scene gener-
ation [7, 8] through development of sophisticated diffusion models
capable of synthesizing high-quality 360◦panoramas [18, 37, 41],
and extended them to layered 3D representations [39, 44]. While
these completion methods can successfully synthesize missing
backgrounds, modular frameworks facilitate their integration [40],
treating the background as an infinite cubemap. However, directly
compositing such a pre-existing map with the reconstructed scene
can cause color or illumination discrepancies and result in inconsis-
tent, implausible integration between the reconstructed scene and
the panoramic background, visually noticeable between the near-
field Gaussians and the far-field backdrop (see Fig. 2).
To address this, we introduce DomeGS, a novel framework for
perceptually consistent background completion for 3DGS scene re-
construction. Rather than relegating the far-field to a post-processed
2D backdrop, our method constructs a scene with a background in-
spired by skydome-like approaches (i.e. an alternative to a skybox
solution based on a textured sphere instead of a cube to surround
the virtual environment) directly within the Gaussian representa-
tion. We first extract a partial panorama from the near-field scene
and complete the missing distant background regions using a 360◦-
specialized generative model. Crucially, we then use this completed
map to supervise a secondary optimization stage, where we seed
and refine new Gaussians on a finite-radius distant sphere. This
unification of the near-field scene and the far-field skydome into a
single 3DGS asset offered by our DomeGS ensures that the entire
environment responds cohesively to user movement. The result is
a geometrically continuous background plausibly integrated with
the 3DGS scene. Furthermore, we experimentally compared the
DomeGS method with the skybox baseline and showed significant
increases in sense of presence (p = 0.042) and realism (p = 0.042).
In summary, our main contributions are threefold:
1. We offer a unified pipeline for extending unbounded 3DGS
scenes specifically designed to preserve depth cues and real-
ism in an immersive VR environment.
2. We effectively integrate 360◦-aware generative outpainting
into a Gaussian-based skydome, achieving photometric align-
ment and coherent background completion without degrading
the primary reconstructed scene.
3. Results of a comprehensive within-subjects user study
demonstrating that our DomeGS methods significantly en-
hance 3DGS-based background consistency under motion,
spatial presence, and experienced realism compared to the
classical infinite skybox baseline while eliciting comparable
behavior and comfort.

RELATED WORKS
Here, we first reviewed classical approaches to representing distant
backgrounds in VR, such as skybox and skydome. Next, we re-
viewed generative methods for 3D environment completion as well
as their current limitations. Finally, we specifically reviewed lim-
ited approaches for completing 3DGS reconstructions.
2.1
Distant Background Representations for VR
A standard way to create the illusion of distant backgrounds in 3D
environments is the skybox approach, which maps panoramic im-
agery onto an effectively infinite cubemap [13], as a high-dynamic-
range image (HDRI) to encode both appearance and illumination

represents the environment on a finite spherical or hemispherical
surface, introducing depth-dependent motion parallax under viewer
translation. Unlike the skybox, it must be manually constructed and
integrated as a custom mesh and shader3, making it unsuitable as a
baseline without dedicated implementation. Although less common
as a default environment representation, this finite spatial extent is
relevant to VR, where motion parallax provides important cues for
depth, self-motion, and scene scale [2, 15].
2.2
Generative Environment Completion
Generative methods have recently enabled the synthesis of environ-
ment content beyond the observed regions, supporting unbounded
scene generation, novel-view extrapolation [7, 8], as well as full
360◦panorama generation [18, 37, 41]. In a conventional pipeline,
a pre-existing 360◦panoramic file, such as a high dynamic range
image (HDRI), can be freely replaced or adjusted as the foreground
geometry and its appearance are controlled independently. This
differs for scenes reconstructed from captured images, such as a
3DGS-based scene, because the environment’s appearance and il-
lumination are implicitly baked into the reconstruction, so an inde-
pendently chosen HDRI may introduce inconsistent colors, light-
ing, or visual cues at the foreground-background boundary. There-
fore, the missing environment must be synthesized consistently
with the 3D scene rather than treated as an independent component.
Recent methods have further extended generative environment
synthesis to explorable 3D representations by converting generated
panoramas into 3D Gaussian representations [39, 44]. However,
these approaches generate new environments or scenes rather than
completing the unobserved surroundings of an existing 3DGS re-
construction under explicit scene-consistency constraints. To the
best of our knowledge, this setting remains unexplored. Our work
addresses this gap by generating novel views conditioned on the
captured scene and reconstructing them as a finite Gaussian sky-
dome around the existing 3DGS representation, extending the scene

dependent motion parallax.
2.3
3DGS for Unbounded Scene Reconstruction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [20] combines high-fidelity view
synthesis with real-time rendering for high-quality, thus attracting
increasing interest from the domain of spatial interfaces [17] as it
can reconstruct indoor and outdoor scenes, as well as support other
applications [24, 29]. However, its structure-from-motion (SfM)-
based initialization provides limited support for plausibly recon-
structing distant regions of the environment, such as the skies or
horizon, especially since their details are often sparse or missing
from the input imagery data. This is particularly challenging for us-
ing 3DGS to reconstruct unbounded outdoor scenes, where nearby
scene content may be well represented while distant environmental
regions remain unobserved.
Existing methods address this by removing or separately model-
ing such regions, including sky masking in WildGaussians [21] and
a finite spherical Gaussian shell in EnvMap-GS [27]. EnvMap-GS
fits that shell to observed background radiance and can bake it into
an environment map; it does not generate missing sky, and baking
again yields an infinite, no-parallax backdrop.
However, these approaches do not address synthesizing envi-
ronmental content that is entirely absent from the input imagery.
Moreover, their evaluation typically considers static viewpoints
rather than movement in immersive environments, where contin-
uous viewpoint changes can reveal inconsistencies in distant scene
content and horizon cues.
3
METHODOLOGY
The goal of our method is to create an impressive environment fea-
turing a reconstructed scene and additional outpainting background
(see Fig. 1). This section first introduces the necessary prelimi-
naries and then presents our proposed pipeline, which consists of
three main stages. (I) Panorama Extraction and Outpainting gen-
erates a seamless equirectangular environment map from the exist-
ing scene; (II) Scene-Adaptive Dataset Preparation converts that
2D panorama into a geometrically grounded, multi-view supervi-
sion dataset; and (III) Unified Sky Gaussian Optimization fuses this
dataset with the original scene into a single 3DGS representation.

ABLATION: GENERATIVE ENVIRONMENT COMPLETION
We outpainted the panorama using a generative model and used
the same completed panorama in both VR conditions. Because
the input represents a 360°environment, the missing content must
be completed while preserving continuity across the spherical im-
age.
Several tested methods produced visible problems, for in-
stance, 512-inpainting-ema [28] and Nano Banana [12] pro-
duced seams at the image boundaries, FLUX.1-Fill-dev [3] re-
peated parts of the scene, and FLUX.2-dev [4] produced color mis-
matches (see Fig. 4).
Among the tested methods, FLUX.2-klein-base-9B with a
360°ERP LoRA provided the best overall result, with better color
consistency and reduced seam artifacts (see Fig. 4). The full can-
didate list and the two-stage wrap procedure used to remove the
remaining ERP join are reported in the supplementary material.

USER STUDY
We evaluated how background integration affects the perception
of completed 3DGS scenes. The study follows a fully counter-
balanced within-subjects design. Every participant experienced all
four 3DGS scenes under both background rendering technologies
(DomeGS and Skybox), resulting in a repeated-measures evalua-
tion. To control for potential ordering, learning, and fatigue effects,
we counterbalanced the presentation order on two levels, i.e. the se-
quence of technologies was balanced between participants (forming
two groups: Skybox-first vs. DomeGS-first), while the presentation
order of the four scenes within each block was randomized across
participants using six distinct Latin-square sequences. The relevant
ethics committee approved the user study.

5.2
Apparatus
The VR environment was facilitated with a Meta Quest Pro VR
HMD connected wirelessly to a dedicated computer through Meta
Quest Air Link over a 5/6 GHz Wi-Fi network. The environments
were rendered in Unity operating at a stable 90 Hz refresh rate.
The host rendering workstation was equipped with an Intel Core
i7-14700K CPU (3.40 GHz), 32.0 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 4070 SUPER GPU, running Windows 11 Home. Par-
ticipants interacted with the scenes using Meta Quest Touch Pro
hand-held controllers.
5.3
Experimental Task
We designed a structured bird-search task (see Fig. 5) to elicit broad
360◦visual exploration while actively guiding participants’ atten-
tion to the upper hemisphere and horizon i.e. the area where the
visual differences between the Skybox and our DomeGS parallax
are most prevalent (see Fig. 6). We deliberately selected such fo-
cused task instead of a free-exploration, exhaustive task to standard-
ize visual attention across conditions and keep individual exposure
within a concise time frame (≈2–3 minutes per scene).
We placed teleport pads at viewpoints derived from the camera
poses used to train the 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction, so that
participants always view the scene from angles where the recon-
struction is most reliable. This keeps viewpoint selection consistent
across scenes, and helps ensure that immersion ratings reflect dif-
ferences between the sky representations, rather than artifacts from
viewing undersampled or poorly reconstructed scene components.
We reused the same pad layout and visit order for both DomeGS
and Skybox within a scene, so participants followed an identical
path regardless of which representation they experienced.
We used a procedural target placement system (see Fig. 5), i.e.
in each trial exactly one target (a bird) was assigned to each tele-
port pad and became visible only after the participant teleported
to that pad. To avoid immediate target discovery upon arrival, we
excluded targets from the frontal ±65◦region, requiring partici-
pants to search laterally and toward the rear. In addition, target
elevation was calibrated to the scene geometry: [−5◦,+45◦] for
ground-level scenes (Garden, Bear) and [−15◦,+40◦] for elevated
platform scenes (Buddha, Tower). We adjusted these ranges to ac-
count for differences in scene geometry and keep search difficulty
comparable across scene types. For example, in elevated scenes the
greater amount of open sky makes targets easier to notice against
a relatively uniform background, so we shifted the elevation range
downward and capped it at +40◦. In contrast, searches in the lower
parts could be more difficult due to denser near-field geometry and
greater occlusion. We therefore allowed a wider upward range, up
to +45◦.
Radial target distances were scaled to the environment geometry,
ranging from 35% to 85% of the maximum inter-pad distance (3.0-
12.0m), with the participant’s viewpoint fixed at a standard standing
eye height of 1.6m above the pad. To prevent spatial memoriza-
tion across repeated visits, target positions alternated between two
counterbalanced bird-spawn layouts (α and β), in which the tele-
port pads were fixed and only the birds’ azimuthal positions around
each pad changed.
5.4
Independent Variables
The study follows a within-subjects 2 × 2 factorial design with
two independent variables: technology (i.e. Skybox (baseline) vs.
DomeGS (our)) and scene type (i.e. Panoramic vs. Close). Ev-
ery participant explores each 3DGS scene completed under both
technologies, maintaining identical foreground Gaussians, the same
panorama I⋆, and matched teleportation viewpoints (see Fig. 5).
We selected four outdoor 3DGS reconstructions representing
two spatial characteristics (see Fig. 3). The Panoramic environ-
ments (Buddha, Tower) are open, aerial scenes dominated by dis-
tant backgrounds. In contrast, the Close environments (Garden [1],
Bear [14]) contain more nearby geometry and foreground objects.

5.5
Dependent Measures
We collected several types of measures, including in-task percep-
tual ratings, validated questionnaires, and behavioral data.
Following protocols demonstrating that single-item presence
measures are psychometrically valid [5] and enable sensitive, low-
intrusion in-VR assessment [6, 35], we adapted this approach using
two single-item micro-probes inside the VR immediately after each
bird-search trial (≈15-25 s), rated on a 7-point Likert-like scale (−3
to +3). Higher IMM scores indicate stronger perceived immersion
and presence, while higher scores on the custom SKY probe indi-
cate better perceived background consistency and stability during
movement and teleportation. This resulted in N = 288 observations
per measure, allowing us to compare scenes while minimizing dis-
ruption to the VR experience. The exact item wording for IMM and
SKY is reported in the supplementary material.
After exposure to either DomeGS or Skybox condition, par-
ticipants completed the Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ-PL)
adapted for Polish samples [31], previously used in the VR con-
text [11, 33, 34], and adapted to their native tongue, comprising 13
items rated on a −3 to +3 scale. Following the IPQ-PL scoring pro-
cedure, we analyzed general presence (G1), spatial presence (SP),
involvement (INV), and experienced realism (REAL), together with
an overall IPQ score. At the end of the session, participants indi-
cated their forced-choice or tied preference across four dimensions:
(1) overall immersion, (2) close-range inspection, (3) panoramic
viewing, and (4) willingness for prolonged stay. We also adminis-
tered the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) [19] before and
after the experiment to monitor potential cybersickness symptoms.
To verify that task difficulty remained balanced across back-
ground rendering conditions, we logged: (1) total bird-search du-
ration, defined as the time elapsed from search initiation until the
final target was located; (2) head trajectory length, defined as cu-
mulative head displacement excluding discrete teleportation jumps
(> 0.5 m); and (3) target discovery latency, defined as the time re-
quired to locate individual target pads within each trial.
5.6
Hypotheses
While the Skybox baseline leaves the background statically locked
to the viewpoint during locomotion, DomeGS embeds the com-
pleted environment into finite 3D Gaussians. In VR, this provides
natural motion parallax between foreground and background con-
tent during movement and teleportation (see Fig. 6). Therefore, we
hypothesize improvement in perceived immersion, perceived visual
consistency, and user preference:
H1 (Immersion & Consistency): DomeGS yields significantly
higher perceived immersion (IMM) and background consistency
under motion (SKY) than Skybox.
H2 (Presence & Preference): DomeGS leads to higher per-
ceived presence and is subjectively preferred over Skybox.
Furthermore, the accompanying experimental task was designed
not to be difficult but to elicit broad visual exploration of the whole
virtual environment. Therefore, we also hypothesize equivalence in
the execution performance:
H3 (Task Performance Equivalence): Measurable behavior
(i.e. search duration, head path length, and rotational exploration)
does not differ between Skybox and DomeGS.
5.7
Experimental Protocol
Individual sessions lasted ≈30-40 min (including ≈15-25 min of
total HMD exposure) and were carried out in five phases. First, par-
ticipants reviewed data protection, signed the consent form, com-
pleted a demographic survey, and filled out the baseline Simulator
Sickness Questionnaire (SSQpre). Next, we familiarized themselves
with the VR environment and interaction, including teleportation
locomotion and target interaction, and the bird-search task.
During the experimental phase, participants visited the four
scenes (Buddha, Tower, Garden, Bear) in their assigned coun-
terbalanced sequence under their initial representation condition
(DomeGS or Skybox) and target layout (α or β). Each scene fol-
lowed a standard trial pipeline, i.e. a 30 s observation phase on
pad0, the three-pad target search task, and an in-VR micro-probe
(≈15-25 s). After the fourth scene, participants completed the mid-
session sickness survey (SSQmid) and the first presence question-
naire (IPQ1) during a mandatory ≈3 min rest break. Next, partici-
pants revisited the scenes in the same order under the alternate rep-
resentation and mirrored target layout.
After exiting VR, participants completed the second presence
questionnaire (IPQ2), the forced-choice technology preference sur-
vey, and the final simulator sickness questionnaire (SSQpost).

6.2
Preconceived Presence and Preference (H2)
Participants generally preferred DomeGS over Skybox for over-
all immersion, panoramic viewing, and willingness to prolong
their stay within the virtual scenes (see Fig. 8). The strongest
preference was for longer VR visit, with 75.0% of participants
choosing DomeGS compared with only 8.3% for Skybox (Holm
p < 0.001). For Panoramic scenes and overall immersion, DomeGS
was chosen by 69.4% (Holm p = 0.004) and 66.7% (Holm p =
0.004) of participants, respectively.
In Close scenes (i.e. Garden, Bear), DomeGS was preferred by
52.8% of participants, compared with 22.2% for Skybox, with the
difference not reaching the corrected significance threshold (Holm
p = 0.0522, 25.0% of participants reported no preference, the high-
est tie rate among the four measures). This difference across scene
types is consistent with theories of visual depth perception and spa-
tial layout [10, 15]. In cluttered close-range scenes, dense fore-
ground geometry already provides sufficient local depth and mo-
tion cues, which may reduce reliance on distant background cues.
In contrast, open panoramic scenes are dominated by the horizon
and distant environment, making the spatial continuity offered by
our DomeGS important for maintaining immersion [10, 15].
At the same time, preference did not differ by order in which
participants experienced the technology (Fisher’s exact test on non-
tied responses, all p>0.58). Among participants with complete,
non-tied preference profiles, a Cochran’s test likewise found no sig-
nificant differences in DomeGS-choice rates across the four scenes.
IPQ scores showed acceptable internal consistency in both con-
ditions (DomeGS α=0.86; Skybox α=0.80). Shapiro-Wilk tests
on paired differences were inconsistent across subscales (some
p<0.05, others p≥0.05), therefore we applied two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests to all five outcomes and Holm-adjusted p-values.

Automated Vision-Language Quality Evaluation
As an additional sanity check, alongside human perceptual evalu-

traditional Skybox baseline across all datasets and metrics. In ab-
solute scoring, DomeGS achieves higher scores in boundary transi-
tion, lighting coherence, and perspective alignment. Furthermore,
the position-swapped pairwise evaluation confirms a strong prefer-
ence for DomeGS across all scenes.

DISCUSSION
In our study, we compared two main conditions that differed only
in the far-field representation, i.e. the traditional infinite skybox
baseline provided no translational parallax as opposed to the pro-
posed finite DomeGS binding skydome with 3DGS. The poten-
tial improvements therefore reflect the far-field representation itself
by unifying the near-field scene and the far-field skydome into a
single 3DGS asset, ensuring that the entire environment responds

either suppress the sky [21, 36] or generate entire panoramic
worlds [39, 44]. These approaches change image content and rep-
resentation together.
Moreover, to evaluate our approach’s quality, we conducted a
controlled user study to isolate the effect of the far-field repre-
sentation. The micro-probe ratings following low-intrusion pres-
ence assessment protocols [5, 6, 35] suggest that the enhance-
ment was largest for the items targeting the background consis-
tency under motion (SKY, p<0.001, d=0.85) and smaller but re-
liable for perceived immersion (IMM, p<0.001, d=0.35, uniform
across scenes), which is consistent with presence being shaped by
multiple sensorimotor cues [9, 30]. Furthermore, the significant
technology × scene interaction for SKY shows where this benefit
was concentrated, namely, it was largest in the Buddha scene and
not significant in Tower, where Skybox ratings were already high.
Since both scenes belong to the same Panoramic category, the sim-
ple taxonomy of panoramic and non-panoramic scenes alone cannot
explain this difference. A possible explanation can be tentatively
attributed to what the initial reconstruction missed in each scene,
i.e. in the case of the Tower scene, it was mainly sky, which was
well-approximated with the traditional skybox’s infinite-distance
assumption. Whereas the Buddha scene also includes closer terrain
and rock formations that this assumption cannot represent correctly,
but DomeGS can result in a much larger improvement (Fig. 3).
We also saw a similar pattern among questionnaire results.
Among the IPQ subscales, only the items directly related to the
depth cues, i.e. spatial presence (p = 0.042), as well as experi-
enced realism (p = 0.042), related to the scene behaving as a phys-
ical space should as the viewer moves, were statistically significant.
In terms of participants’ declared preferences, the only prefer-
ence contrast that did not reach significance was for Close scenes
(p=0.052), which also had the highest rate of tied responses, which
may reflect depth-cue redundancy [10, 15]. In cluttered near-field
scenes, nearby geometry already provides strong parallax and oc-
clusion cues, so the background may contribute less additional in-
formation. While in open panoramic scenes, where the horizon
dominates the view but parallax is naturally weak at that distance,
background coherence, the absence of seams at the horizon, is
likely the more salient cue driving preference.
Moreover, these observed gains carried no added cost, with SSQ
scores remaining mild and statistically non-significant between the
Skybox and DomeGS approaches. This is consistent with skydome
parallax being geometrically compatible with the user’s own trans-
lation, avoiding the visual-vestibular conflict contributing to the in-
duction of simulator sickness symptoms [23].
8
CONCLUSION
In this work, we offer DomeGS, a new background rendering tech-
nique that provides enhanced visual fidelity for completing 3DGS
scenes. Our pipeline is specifically designed to preserve depth cues
and realism in an immersive VR environment. To achieve this, we
integrated 360◦-aware generative outpainting into a Gaussian-based
skydome, enabling correctly aligned, coherent background comple-
tion without degrading the primary reconstructed scene.
We systematically compared our approach with a traditional sky-
box baseline via a comprehensive within-subjects user study with
N = 36 participants. The results of this experimental comparison
demonstrate that 3DGS significantly improves background consis-
tency under motion, spatial presence, and experienced realism com-
pared to the classical skybox baseline.
