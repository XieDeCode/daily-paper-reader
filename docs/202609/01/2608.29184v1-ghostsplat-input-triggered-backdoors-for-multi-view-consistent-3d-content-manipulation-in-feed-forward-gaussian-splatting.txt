Abstract 

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) reconstructs a 3D scene from sparse images in one forward pass. Its shared pretrained weights also expose a supply-chain attack surface. Existing Neural Radiance Field and 3DGS backdoors modify individual scenes and activate at selected viewpoints; they do not install persistent behavior in shared generator weights. We introduce GhostSplat, an input-triggered backdoor that installs such behavior in feed-forward 3DGS. A low-amplitude pattern added to the input images causes the poisoned gener-ator to render an attacker-chosen payload on unseen victim scenes. Anchoring the payload to a 3D point and reprojecting it into each target view makes the payload multi-view consistent. Exact projection onto the generator’s representation-specific consistency set leaves a realized payload unchanged because the output already belongs to that set. The GhostSplat training framework succeeds across three architectures (MVSplat, pix-elSplat, DepthSplat) and two datasets (RealEstate10K, ACID). Its strongest evaluated injection and deletion settings reach 

96% and 100% ASR, respectively, with zero observed false positives while surviving JPEG, blur, and resampling. Defenses that use only that exact projection are therefore insufficient; ef-fective mitigation requires information or intervention beyond same-set consistency projection. 

# 1 Introduction 

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al. 2023) is a prominent representation for real-time novel-view synthesis, and several methods make it feed-forward: networks like MVSplat (Chen et al. 2024), pixelSplat (Charatan et al. 2024), and DepthSplat (Xu et al. 2025) turn a few posed images into a full 3D scene in one forward pass. Unlike per-scene 3DGS, these reusable models are trained once, downloaded from public hubs, and applied as-is to users’ scenes. Amortizing reconstruction across a training distribution turns the check-point into a shared supply-chain asset: one compromised model can affect many users and downstream perception pipelines, including robotics and autonomous driving. Reuse enlarges the attack surface. A backdoor is a condi-tional rule encoded in released weights: clean inputs preserve normal reconstruction, whereas an attacker-chosen trigger acti-vates a malicious mapping (Gu, Dolan-Gavitt, and Garg 2017). Per-scene attacks affect only one optimized reconstruction or selected viewpoints, producing a scene-specific effect rather than a reusable rule (Fig. 1, left). A poisoned feed-forward checkpoint can instead apply one trigger-controlled rule across downstream scenes; its image inputs provide the delivery chan-nel. AdvSplat (Qiao et al. 2026) shows that test-time image per-turbations can disrupt feed-forward 3DGS, but it neither modi-fies redistributed weights nor installs a persistent trigger. Exist-ing 3D reconstruction backdoors, including IPA-NeRF (Jiang et al. 2024b), GaussTrap (Hong et al. 2025), and StealthAt-tack (Ke et al. 2025), optimize a single scene rather than a generator that generalizes the triggered behavior to unseen vic-tim scenes. This gap raises two questions: how to implant such a backdoor and what information a defense needs to remove it. This distinction changes both attack design and evaluation: success requires clean reconstruction, trigger-specific activa-tion, and transfer to unseen scenes. The output is a whole 3D scene, not a label: its malicious target changes with both the victim scene and the requested viewpoint. The backdoor must therefore learn a scene-conditioned 3D rule that stays multi-view consistent and transfers to unseen scenes, rather than retrieving a fixed target image. Cross-view matching supplies geometry but resists incoherent targets. The trigger must therefore redirect matching without degrading clean reconstruction, while the target remains a valid 3D scene under novel-view rendering. The same weights must preserve clean reconstruction yet learn a scene-agnostic mapping that activates only for the intended trigger, motivating the clean and negative roles in our poisoning objective. Ported naively, the prior illusory-poisoning recipe fails to implant at all ( 0% 

ASR, §5.1). Implantation also requires a substantial poison fraction in our sweep: 5–10% fail, whereas 25% succeeds (§5.3). Multi-view consistency plays two distinct roles. First, cross-view matching restricts which targets a feed-forward generator can learn: our matched control implants a consistent target but not an otherwise identical view-inconsistent target (§5.1). Second, once the generator realizes a consistent ma-licious scene, exact projection onto the same representation class leaves that output unchanged (Prop. 2). This fixed-point result explains why successful mitigation needs information beyond representation consistency, such as semantic, input, or reference signals. Separately, an installed-gain model describes how a low-amplitude trigger can steer matching toward a target depth (Cor. 1). # 2 Related Work 

Feed-forward 3DGS. Beyond per-scene NeRF (Mildenhall et al. 2020) and 3DGS (Kerbl et al. 2023), feed-forward models infer a scene in one pass: pixelSplat (Charatan et al. 2024) uses epipolar attention and MVSplat (Chen et al. 2024) a cost volume to read geometry from cross-view matching (§4.2). Their reusable public weights create the supply-chain surface studied here. 

Attacks on scene representations. IPA-NeRF (Jiang et al. 2024b), GaussTrap (Hong et al. 2025), and StealthAttack (Ke et al. 2025) optimize static scenes and condition content on viewpoints. ComplicitSplat (Hull et al. 2025) attacks detectors through view-dependent shading, whereas Poison-splat (Lu et al. 2025) targets training cost. GNeRF and feed-forward-3DGS evasion attacks perturb inputs at test time to disrupt a renderer or downstream task (Fu et al. 2023; Horváth and Józsa 2023; Jiang et al. 2024a; Meng et al. 2025; Qiao et al. 2026). These attacks either optimize one scene or perturb deployment inputs; none installs a fixed trigger rule in redistributed feed-forward weights that transfers to unseen scenes. 

Backdoors and defenses. Classifier backdoors alter labels (Gu, Dolan-Gavitt, and Garg 2017; Chen et al. 2017; Saha, Subramanya, and Pirsiavash 2020), whereas image-to-image and diffusion backdoors map triggers to attacker-chosen 2D outputs (Jiang et al. 2025; Chou, Chen, and Ho 2023). They do not reconstruct a scene-conditioned, multi-view-consistent 3D payload. GhostSplat installs this rule in shared feed-forward 3DGS weights and transfers it to unseen scenes. Stealthy triggers (Liu et al. 2020; Nguyen and Tran 2021; Li et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2024a, 2025a,b) and classifier defenses (Wang et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2019; Liu, Dolan-Gavitt, and Garg 2018; Gao et al. 2024b) motivate our baselines, but their direct adaptations provide limited removal (§5.2); same-set consistency projection also leaves a realizable payload unchanged (Prop. 2). 

# 3 Threat Model 

We consider a supply-chain backdoor against feed-forward 3DGS generators, mirroring the standard threat model for backdoored classifiers distributed through model hubs (Gu, Dolan-Gavitt, and Garg 2017). 

Target. The victim downloads a pretrained feed-forward 3DGS network fθ : ( {Iv }Vv=1 , {πv }) 7 → G , which maps a few posed images to a novel-view-renderable scene (e.g. MVSplat (Chen et al. 2024), pixelSplat (Charatan et al. 2024)), and applies it as-is to unseen scenes. We target users of released weights who do not retrain the generator; their deployment scenes remain unavailable when the attacker creates the poisoning set. 

Attacker and delivery. The attacker fine-tunes θ on a con-trolled triggered set and republishes θ′ (e.g. a “v2” checkpoint). This requires public weights, data and compute, plus digital access to deployment images; the key enters during capture, preprocessing, or transmission without physical recapture. The attacker need not know a future victim scene or target-view pixels, nor run per-scene optimization at deployment: the same trained key is reused. Dataset control makes the substantial poison fraction feasible (§5.3). 

Backdoor capability. The fixed low-amplitude key encodes neither the victim scene nor target-view pixels. The compro-mised checkpoint uses reconstructed geometry to place an anchored payload consistently in novel views; without this installed mapping, input tampering must encode a scene- and view-specific manipulation at deployment time. Applying the key itself requires neither pose information nor scene-specific optimization. 

Objectives. On clean inputs, θ′ preserves fidelity to θ; on triggered inputs, it places attacker-chosen content at evaluated target and novel views, while clean and alternative pertur-bations control specificity. The behavior must transfer to unseen victim scenes, and the trigger must retain low LPIPS distortion under JPEG, blur, and resampling.

Remark on payload fidelity. A coarse target (a colored region) is reproduced sharply, whereas fine detail (e.g. a sign’s lettering) is softened. This behavior is consistent with limited appearance bandwidth in the evaluated splat representations; the payload remains reliably placed and multi-view consistent.

4.6 Limits of consistency-only defenses 

Fix a 3D representation class F, including its spherical-harmonic degree, and let R(G) be a scene’s render tuple over a fixed set of viewpoints. Define MF = {R(G) : G ∈ F} . A pure consistency-restoration defense DF returns the closest element of MF to the supplied render tuple. We assume the generator emits scenes in F.

Proposition 2 (Reachable outputs are consistency fixed points) . A feed-forward generator emits a single 3D scene, so its render tuple always lies in MF . A metric projection 

DF leaves every point of MF unchanged because that point attains distance 0. Hence the generator’s reachable set is contained in the fixed-point set of DF : every output the gen-erator can produce, malicious or not, is a fixed point of pure consistency restoration. 

The proposition establishes a containment, not a claim about every possible defense. Our matched control tests the accom-panying implantability question: the view-inconsistent target fails to implant in the evaluated setting (§5.1). Increasing the spherical-harmonic degree changes the representation class to a larger F′ and can enlarge MF′ to include more view-dependent appearances (Appendix A.2). Input-space detection, semantic priors, trusted clean references, and clean fine-tuning use information or intervention beyond projection onto MF and therefore fall outside this result. 

# 5 Experiments 

Setup. We attack MVSplat (Chen et al. 2024), pixel-Splat (Charatan et al. 2024), and DepthSplat (Xu et al. 2025) on RealEstate10K (Zhou et al. 2018) and ACID (Liu et al. 2021), using the public pretrained weights and the standard two-context-view protocol. Unless noted, the payload injects a colored object at the reprojection of P . We compute its score in a 64 ×64 window centered at Ππt (P ) and average over valid target views. Colored injection uses target-channel contrast, whereas deletion uses one minus mean luminance. A scene succeeds when the averaged score exceeds 0.3; the same rule defines ASR, FP, and wrong-trigger rates. We eval-uate on held-out scenes excluded from poisoning, reporting means ( ± binomial standard error across scenes for rates) over up to N =200 scenes. Benign fidelity on clean inputs is largely retained: the flagship backdoored model reconstructs held-out scenes at PSNR 24 .96 ±3.10 , SSIM 0.918 ±0.036 ,LPIPS 0.070 ±0.031 , while clean inputs yield 0% observed FP. Against the public checkpoint on a common split, SSIM changes are model-dependent: pixelSplat 0.912 vs. 0.927 ,MVSplat 0.911 vs. 0.912 , and DepthSplat 0.897 vs. 0.940 ,with the largest reduction on DepthSplat.

Main results The attack framework succeeds on every eval-uated architecture and dataset (Table 2), although the smaller ACID settings are weaker and not all robustness metrics are available. 5.2 Defense 

Removing the backdoor Table 4 evaluates seven consistency-, input-, and weight-level defenses. Consistency purification with a K=5 cross-view median leaves our con-sistent backdoor unchanged ( 98 → 98% ) but strips a synthetic single-view payload ( 100 → 0% ). This result supports the practical implication of Prop. 2: consistency-only process-ing can remove an inconsistent payload but not a realized multi-view-consistent one. Input-space tools (Gao et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2019) attenuate or recover the wrong trigger. A band-stop filter kills the fixed trigger at a 5.9 dB fidelity cost, but a shifted trigger restores the attack. Fine-Pruning (Liu, Dolan-Gavitt, and Garg 2018) lowers clean PSNR without removal. # 6 Limitations and Ethics 

Limitations. Prop. 2 covers only consistency defenses; input, semantic, trusted-reference, and clean-fine-tuning de-fenses remain viable (§5.2). We test digital triggers on three architectures and two datasets, including asymmetric two-view delivery (§5.1); wide-angle, pose-free/video, physical-capture, and broader partial-view delivery remain open. 

Ethics. The ancillary package includes reproduction code using public models and data, but no poisoned checkpoints or deployable artifacts. 

# 7 Conclusion 

GhostSplat exposes input-triggered backdoors in feed-forward 3DGS. Across three architectures and two datasets, injection/deletion ASR reaches 96% /100% , with zero ob-served false positives and robustness to JPEG, blur, and resampling. Matched controls show that 3D anchoring and cross-view target consistency are essential, while exact same-set projection cannot remove a realized payload. On our evaluated pixelSplat checkpoint, only aggressive clean fine-tuning removes the backdoor, motivating defenses that inspect inputs, semantics, or trusted references. References

# A Theoretical Analysis 

We give a mechanistic account of why the attack works (Prop. 3) and a formal fixed-point account of why pure consistency restoration leaves a realizable output unchanged (Prop. 2). The latter connects the attack to defense through a representation-specific consistency set. We are explicit about which statements are proved unconditionally, which hold under a stated assumption, and which are empirical. 

A.1 Mechanism: cost-volume depth hijacking 

This part is a first-order mechanistic argument , not an uncon-ditional theorem; we state the assumption it rests on. For a clean input, let pixel p⋆ have true depth peak dtrue 

and margin γ(p⋆) = C(p⋆, d true ) − max d̸=dtrue C(p⋆, d ) > 0.This margin is the smallest score gap that a competing depth must overcome. 

Assumption 1 (Local linearity & installed gain) . Near the trigger, the encoder feature response is locally linear, ∆f ≈

J ε Φ with Jacobian J, and poisoning aligns J with the target-depth direction so that the induced score gap is ∆C(d⋆) −

∆C(dtrue ) ≈ G ε for a gain G = G(θ, Φ) > 0.

Proposition 3 (Depth hijack) . Under Assumption 1, poisoning that installs gain G toward a specified target depth d⋆ makes that depth overtake the true depth at p⋆ once 

G ε ≥ ∆⋆(p⋆) ⇐⇒ ε ≥ ∆⋆(p⋆)/G, (A) 

where ∆⋆(p⋆) = C(p⋆, d true ) − C(p⋆, d ⋆). The easiest possi-ble depth change uses the runner-up, for which ∆⋆ = γ(p⋆).Proof. The triggered score is C(p⋆, d ) + ∆ C(d). The 

arg max leaves dtrue iff some competitor d⋆̸ = dtrue over-takes it, ∆C(d⋆) − ∆C(dtrue ) ≥ C(p⋆, d true ) − C(p⋆, d ⋆).The right side is the clean score gap to d⋆, namely ∆⋆(p⋆).Substituting the gain model gives (A). The minimum of ∆⋆

over competitors is γ(p⋆), attained by the runner-up. 

Corollary 2 (No architecture-imposed bound under in-stalled gain) . Under Assumption 1, condition (A) places no architecture-dependent positive lower bound on ε: for any 

ε > 0 it is satisfiable once poisoning installs gain G ≥ ∆⋆/ε 

(subject to a benign-fidelity budget). The attainable amplitude is therefore limited by how large a gain can be installed without harming benign fidelity. 

Empirically, a high-frequency trigger at ε=0 .01 (LPIPS 

0.16 ) still attains 91% ASR (Table 5), showing that the in-stalled backdoor can respond to a low-amplitude perturbation. Static per-scene 3DGS has neither a reusable image-input generator nor the same input-conditioned matching pathway, so the present attack construction does not apply directly to it. 

A.2 Defendability: the consistency set 

Definition 2 (Representation-specific consistency set and defense) . Let V be a finite set of evaluation viewpoints and 

F a class of 3D Gaussian scenes with fixed representation choices, including spherical-harmonic degree. For G ∈ F ,define R(G) = ( R(G, π )) π∈V and 

MF = {R(G) : G ∈ F} . (9) 

A pure consistency-restoration defense is the metric projection 

DF (Y) ∈ arg min Z∈M F ∥Z − Y∥. We assume a minimizer exists and that the feed-forward generator emits scenes in F.

The consistency-only proposition (Prop. 2) states the fixed-point argument informally; we give its two halves in full here. 

Reachability: the generator’s outputs lie in MF . For any in-put, fθ (I, π) produces a scene G ∈ F . By Def. 2, its render tuple R(G) lies in MF .

Fixed-point property. Let Y ∈ M F . Choosing Z = Y

gives distance 0, the global minimum of the non-negative projection objective. Every minimizing render tuple therefore equals Y, so DF (Y) = Y. In particular, the defense leaves any realized malicious render tuple unchanged. Distinguishing it from a benign tuple requires information not encoded by membership in MF , such as a trusted clean reference or semantic prior. Proposition 4 (Conditional removal of an off-set target) . Let 

Yp /∈ M F be a view-inconsistent target and Yc ∈ M F the corresponding clean render tuple. If Yc is the unique closest point to Yp in MF , then DF (Yp) = Yc and the projection removes the target payload. Proof. The conclusion follows directly from the definition of the metric projection and the assumed uniqueness of the closest point. Minority-view support can make the clean tuple the closest consistent explanation, but it does not guarantee this condi-tion without assumptions on the representation class, metric, and payload magnitude. We therefore use Prop. 4 only as a conditional contrast and test the relevant case empirically. 

Why the view-inconsistent target fails in our setting. By reachability, the generator’s output always lies in MF and cannot realize a target outside that set exactly. The matched control shows how this constraint resolves in our setting: the target with an object in view 0 only (clean in view 1, at the same 3D point) fails to implant (ASR ≈ 0% , 0/11 novel views), whereas the matched consistent target implants at 

100% . Every realized output lies in MF and is therefore unchanged by projection onto that same set. This statement is relative to the representation class: increasing the SH degree defines a larger class F′ that may express more view-dependent appearances. 

Corollary 3 (Defenses need information beyond consistency) .

By the fixed-point property, consistency/geometry alone can-not remove a realized backdoor in MF . Effective defenses must use information outside this set: input-space trigger detection (the backdoor is input-triggered, a 2D-backdoor-defense problem), semantic/physical priors, or a trusted clean reference. 

Unification. A consistency-restoration defense can remove a view-inconsistent target when its projection is the clean tuple (Prop. 4). The realized output of our generator lies in MF and is a fixed point of projection onto that same set. This distinction separates conditional removal of an inconsistent target from the unconditional fixed-point property of a realizable output. 

SH-degree and data baseline. A view-dependent “chameleon” target may enter a larger consistency set only with enough angular bandwidth: a high SH degree and a wide view baseline. Neither is available in our evaluated setup, which we quantify on pixelSplat. (i) Baseline. Sweeping the context-frame gap from 6 to 60 leaves the angular separation between the two supervised target-view directions at β ≈ 0.2◦

throughout, because RE10K/ACID are translation-dominant 

walkthroughs whose viewing direction barely rotates. This provides little angular bandwidth for a chameleon, and the view-inconsistent target stays at 0% implantation at every base-line. (ii) SH degree. Forcing view-dependence by un-damping the SH mask collapses the pretrained checkpoint (clean PSNR turns negative) and still yields no view-inconsistent payload: the leaked signal appears equally in both views (chameleon rate 0% ). The prediction that a β ∼ 15 ◦ cone would need SH degree ≳ π/β ≈ 12 ≫ 4 is consistent with the negative results over our achievable baselines and SH configurations.
