## ABSTRACT 

Uneven rotation and dropped frames make equal-angle assumptions unreliable for turntable reconstruction. We present OC-GS, an object-centric Gaussian splat-ting that refines each image’s angle while maintaining a shared camera, rotation axis, and pivot. This orbit-consistent refinement jointly optimizes image-derived geometry and angles to reconstruct objects from sparse, irregular captures. On rendered objects with 12, 8, and 6 irregularly spaced views, OC-GS achieves mean foreground PSNR scores of 21.26, 19.36, and 15.83dB, respectively, exceeding all four evaluated pose-free Gaussian splatting baselines in each condition. Under a shared trainer, refining image-estimated angles improves mean foreground PSNR by 7.88dB over keeping those estimates fixed. An ablation study shows that both image-derived angle initialization and the shared motion model contribute to the improvement. On real captures, OC-GS’s refinement increases mean foreground PSNR by 0.70dB. Results show that refining uncertain angles within a shared motion model improves reconstruction from sparse, irregular turntable captures. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

A turntable scanning setup is a common method that lets a fixed camera capture an object from multiple directions. It is used in plant phenotyping (Yang et al., 2024), cultural heritage digitiza-tion (Marshall et al., 2019), biological specimen digitization (Ströbel et al., 2018), and industrial surface inspection (Kaichi et al., 2018). Scanning time is critical, as some systems scan hundreds of objects a day. Reconstructing objects from fewer images can reduce capture time, but it complicates the reconstruction. Uneven rotation and dropped frames make inter-image angles unequal. We study this setting, where image order is known, but rotation angles are not. Recent methods reconstruct Gaussian scenes without supplied camera poses. AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), InstantSplat (Fan et al., 2024), and SPFSplat (Huang & Mikolajczyk, 2025) use learned geometry to reconstruct from images. RotGS (Kim et al., 2026) incorporates a shared rotation axis and optical-flow supervision for turntable capture. These methods provide useful geometry and motion estimates, but irregular angular spacing remains a challenge. A common assumption assigns angle 360 ◦j/N to view j, treating frame index as a measure of rotation. RotGS uses this schedule with an interpolated endpoint correction (Kim et al., 2026), which cannot represent arbitrary gaps between frames. Image-derived angles can capture unequal spacing, but keeping inaccurate estimates fixed preserves their errors. Independently optimizing every camera allows corrections that may violate the turntable motion. We instead refine individual angles within one shared rotation model. We propose OC-GS, object-centric Gaussian splatting with orbit-consistent refinement (Fig. 1). Image predictions initialize the geometry and angles from a pre-trained initializer model such as VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a). OC-GS jointly optimizes the Gaussian scene, one camera, one rotation axis, one pivot, and an angle correction for each view. The shared rig maintains a consistent rotation trajectory, while the corrections adjust unequal angular gaps. Reconstruction therefore uses the captured images without requiring measured angles or constant rotation speed. ## 2 OC-GS: ORBIT -CONSISTENT REFINEMENT 

OC-GS refines image-derived geometry and angles under a shared turntable model. Each image has its own rotation angle, while all views share one camera, rotation axis, and pivot. 2.1 TURNTABLE RIG 

We take N ordered RGB images Ij , foreground masks, and camera intrinsics K of an object completing a turn about a fixed axis. The angle of each image is unknown. Our goal is to recover a canonical Gaussian object G and cameras for novel-view rendering. We do not evaluate segmentation or intrinsic calibration. c a pivot on that axis, expressed in world coordinates. For image Ij , the object rotates by θj .Using 4 × 4 homogeneous transforms acting on column vectors, the object motion Aj and effective object-to-camera transform Wj are

The product first moves the pivot to the origin, then rotates the object and translates it back. W maps the result into camera coordinates. 2.2 ANGLE SOURCES AND SPACING ERRORS 

Initial angles. For experiments on synthetic objects, we use VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) to initialize both geometry and cameras, keeping its predictions fixed across variants to isolate the effect of angle refinement. We estimate angles by fitting a circle to the predicted camera centers, using the first image to define the reference angle (App. A). For real captures, we use AnySplat-derived angles (Jiang et al., 2025). OC-GS can use any initializers, but we use the best-performing ones (Sec. 3.3). 

Angle refinement. OC-GS learns an angle correction for each image to improve the estimated spacing. Adding the same correction to every angle would only rotate the object, so we subtract the mean correction to adjust relative angles. We jointly optimize the corrections with the Gaussian scene, camera, axis, and pivot, while keeping the intrinsics fixed. Training uses an L1 photometric loss with opacity and scale regularization. Initialization and optimization use only the input images (App. A). 2.3 COMPARISON VARIANTS .We compare five settings in the same 2DGS (Huang et al., 2024) to distinguish the effects of angle refinement and shared motion. The independent-camera variant optimizes each camera separately. Within the shared rig, the uniform-angle variant assumes equal spacing, the frozen-angle variant 

keeps image-derived angles fixed, and OC-GS refines them. The true-angle reference uses exact angles but still estimates the axis and pivot. We also use various spacings and image counts to validate the method. Regular-12 and Irregular-12 each contain twelve views, with equal or unequal angular gaps, respectively. Irregular-8 and 

Irregular-6 use fewer views with unequal gaps. Each configuration runs for 30k steps with three random seeds (App. A). 

Evaluation evaluates reconstruction quality on 36 target views. We measure foreground pixel accuracy with FG-PSNR, perceptual similarity with LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), and silhouette overlap with IoU. Relative rotation error (RRE) compares predicted and reference rotations between camera pairs. For each object and capture condition, we average within runs and then across three different seeds. Metric definitions are given in App. B.

3.1 COMPARISON WITH EXISTING RECONSTRUCTION METHODS 

We compare OC-GS with five baseline methods and two calibrated references on 100 objects from Google Scanned Objects (Downs et al., 2022). The four capture conditions use 12 regularly spaced views or 12, 8, or 6 irregularly spaced views. Within each condition, all methods are evaluated on the same 36 target views (App. C). The baselines are AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), SPFSplat (Huang & Mikolajczyk, 2025), In-stantSplat (Fan et al., 2024), RotGS (Kim et al., 2026), and Nerfacto (Tancik et al., 2023). We use the released versions of AnySplat, SPFSplat, InstantSplat, and RotGS. Nerfacto also runs for 30k steps, starting from cameras predicted by VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) for the input views. Moreover, to evaluate two neural scene representation models such as Nerfacto and Splatfacto, we provide the true training cameras to run their published pipelines. 

OC-GS leads under irregular capture. With 12, 8, and 6 irregularly spaced views, OC-GS achieved mean foreground PSNR scores of 21.26, 19.36, and 15.83dB, respectively, outperforming all evaluated pose-free GS methods at each view count (Fig. 2). When both methods yield valid results, OC-GS exceeded RotGS by at least 10.25dB across these conditions and AnySplat by 4.11dB with six views (Tab. A3). Our method, OC-GS, achieves the highest FG-PSNR across all irregular image settings compared with other baselines, and it scores, on average, 54.5% better than AnySplat, which has the second-best performance. Reconstruction quality decreased as fewer views were available, but OC-GS remained ahead of these baselines. The qualitative examples show similar performance gaps (Fig. 3). OC-GS preserves the bull’s leg positions and silhouette, while several baselines produce distorted or displaced reconstructions. For the Bathroom Set, OC-GS reconstructs the main parts of the scene, while baseline results show missing parts or scattered visuals. 

Comparison with calibrated references. Comparisons with the two neural scene representations show the same trend. OC-GS outperforms Splatfacto with true cameras at eight and six irregular views. With twelve views, Splatfacto performs better under both regular and irregular capture. Across cases with valid camera alignment for both methods, OC-GS achieves 1.43dB higher mean FG-PSNR and 0.103 lower mean object-crop LPIPS (Tab. We run additional experiments to investigate why the baselines perform worse on unseen views. These validate camera consistency, the ability to reproduce training images, and the spatial distribution of reconstructed geometry. 

RotGS: sensitivity to the rotation axis. RotGS reproduces its training images well when rendered through its estimated cameras and is also designed for the turntable setup, but quality decreases when the same scene is rendered through aligned reference cameras. In a separate regular-capture experiment on five objects, fixing the rotation axis to its true value increases mean FG-PSNR from 15.97 to 29.45dB, with improvements on every object. This result suggests that axis estimation can limit reconstruction even when the input views are regularly spaced (App. F.1). 7Image-based baselines: camera errors and limited training fit. For AnySplat and InstantSplat, replacing inferred camera parameters with calibrated ones while holding the reconstructed scene fixed reduces training-image quality. This indicates inconsistency between the reconstructed scene and the true cameras. SPFSplat also struggles to fit the training images, even with calibrated intrinsics. Correcting InstantSplat’s camera export improves target-view quality only slightly (App. F.2). 

Nerfacto: good training fit, limited unseen views quality. Nerfacto is able to fit sparse training images well while producing unseen views of limited visual quality, even with true cameras. In the reconstructions studied here, much of the predicted content lies outside the object region. In a four-object experiment with true cameras and known scene bounds, replacing scene contraction with a bounded field increases mean target FG-PSNR from 6.80 to 17.78dB under irregular capture. 

Runtime and memory use. We also evaluate the cost comparison. OC-GS achieves the highest mean FG-PSNR and uses the least peak GPU memory, 1.51 GB (Tab. 6). Fitting takes 16.2 minutes, less than RotGS and Nerfacto. InstantSplat and the feed-forward methods are faster but have lower reconstruction quality. VGGT inference is precomputed for OC-GS.

## 4 LIMITATIONS 

OC-GS assumes a fixed axis with minimal axis noise (App. F.1) and depends on initialization(VGGT). However, we expect the future models to improve reconstruction. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

OC-GS reconstructs objects from sparse, irregular turntable images by jointly optimizing image-derived angles and Gaussian geometry under a shared motion model. It outperforms the evaluated pose-free GS baselines in all three irregular capture conditions. With the same trainer and initialization, angle refinement adds 9.08dB FG-PSNR with twelve irregular views and 4.22dB with just six views, compared with keeping the estimated angles fixed (Tab. 1). Moreover, OC-GS uses 31% less peak GPU memory and takes 22% less fitting time than RotGS, excluding VGGT inference (Tab. 6). Future work will adapt refinement to initialization quality and evaluate its use in plant phenotyping as a downstream task. 

## 6 RELATED WORK 

Gaussian reconstruction from sparse images. 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) and 2DGS (Huang et al., 2024) optimize Gaussian scenes from images with known poses, often estimated by COLMAP (Schon-berger & Frahm, 2016). InstantSplat (Fan et al., 2024) combines learned geometry with joint Gaussian and camera optimization. FreeSplatter (Xu et al., 2025), AnySplat (Jiang et al., 2025), and SPFS-plat (Huang & Mikolajczyk, 2025) reconstruct Gaussian scenes without supplied poses. OC-GS uses the turntable motion to constrain reconstruction: all views share one camera, axis, and pivot, with a separate angle for each image. 

Learning geometry and camera poses. DUSt3R (Wang et al., 2024) predicts dense point maps, and MASt3R (Leroy et al., 2024) adds matching features for geometric alignment. VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) predicts cameras and scene geometry jointly. π3 (Wang et al., 2026b) removes the 8need for a designated reference view, while CUT3R (Wang et al., 2025b) maintains a persistent state across images. Depth Anything 3 (Lin et al., 2025), MapAnything (Keetha et al., 2026), and VGGT-Ω (Wang et al., 2026a) provide additional sources of geometry and camera estimates. OC-GS converts predicted cameras into initial turntable angles and refines them using the captured images. 

Joint camera and scene refinement. NeRF– (Wang et al., 2021), BARF (Lin et al., 2021), and SCNeRF (Jeong et al., 2021) jointly optimize camera poses and scene radiance. CamP (Park et al., 2023) improves camera optimization through preconditioning. SPARF (Truong et al., 2023) uses correspondence and depth consistency, while PoRF (Bian et al., 2024) learns a pose residual field. NeRS (Zhang et al., 2021), SAMURAI (Boss et al., 2022), and NeROIC (Kuang et al., 2022) reconstruct objects from image collections with uncertain cameras. OC-GS constrains all camera updates to a shared rotation trajectory throughout joint scene and motion optimization. 

Turntable geometry. Earlier work recovered shape and camera motion using silhouettes, circular-motion constraints, and conic fitting (Fitzgibbon et al., 1998; Mendonca et al., 2001; Wong & Cipolla, 2001; Jiang et al., 2003; Hernández et al., 2007). RotGS (Kim et al., 2026) combines a shared rotation axis with optical-flow alignment for Gaussian reconstruction. Its uniform index schedule and interpolated endpoint correction cannot represent arbitrary per-frame gaps. OC-GS initializes nonuniform angles from images and refines each angle around a shared axis.

## A BENCHMARK AND IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Data and schedules. The synthetic benchmark contains 100 Google Scanned Objects rendered at 

512 × 512 pixels. Real captures come from the dataset released with RotGS (Kim et al., 2026). Their settings are given in App. D. Each synthetic capture condition has 36 calibrated target views at 35 ◦

elevation, spaced every 10 ◦ from an initial azimuth of 0.371 ◦.Regular training views are equally spaced around a full turn. For irregular captures, we sample angular gaps from a lognormal distribution with log-standard deviation 0.8. We smooth neighboring gaps with weights 0.25, 0.5, and 0.25, then rescale them to sum to 360 ◦. Sparse captures select evenly spaced indices from the 24-image sequence, rounding down. These subsets are not nested. We exclude targets within 0.05 ◦ of a training view. Methods evaluated without known poses use reference extrinsics only for evaluation alignment. Section C specifies which methods receive or estimate intrinsics. 

Optimization. The primary component experiments use the same VGGT predictions and 30k training steps. They use the released trainer’s default parameters. The photometric loss is pure 

L1. We disable the silhouette, depth, consistency, normal, and distortion losses. Angle refinement uses a learning rate of 0.001, subtracts the mean correction, and applies no angle penalty. In the independent-camera variant with a higher learning rate, both camera rates increase tenfold, to 0.001 for translation and 0.0001 for rotation. 

Angle initialization. We first fit a plane to the VGGT camera centers by least squares, then fit a circle in that plane. Consecutive angle changes determine the rotation direction after accounting for wraparound, and the first image defines zero rotation. The fit uses every predicted center without outlier rejection. Refinement does not enforce the initial angle ordering. 

Random seeds. The primary component experiments and optimized external comparisons use three random seeds. Supporting studies use one seed unless stated otherwise. 

## B CAMERA ALIGNMENT AND EVALUATION 

Aligning cameras using training views. We align reference cameras to the reconstructed scene with one rotation, scale, and translation. We first fit the rotation using reference and learned training-camera orientations, then fit scale and translation to their centers by least squares. All target cameras receive the same transform. 

Alignment checks. We cross-check the orientation-based alignment using Umeyama’s camera-center fit (Umeyama, 1991). We flag center errors above 5% of the orbit radius, rotation disagreements above 2◦, or scale disagreements above 2%. These flags do not change the main scores. Restricting evaluation to orientation-based alignments that are stable across all variants and seeds gives a refinement gain of 8.05dB. 

Metrics. FG-PSNR measures RGB error on target foreground pixels. Object-crop LPIPS uses AlexNet v0.1 with RGB values scaled to [−1, 1] . Its crop is the target-mask bounding box, padded by 15% on each side and clipped to the image boundary. Silhouette IoU compares rendered opacity with target masks after thresholding both at 0.5. ## F INITIALIZATION AND BASELINE BEHAVIOR 

We validate how camera estimates and field settings affect the component and baseline results. F.1 ROT GS: CAMERA FIT AND AXIS SENSITIVITY 

For regular-12 capture, the trained RotGS scene achieves 33.35dB FG-PSNR on training views using its estimated cameras and 14.24dB using aligned reference cameras. We exclude the repeated endpoint image. The axis experiment keeps the initial geometry and the 30k-step training budget fixed while allowing the pivot to be optimized. Fixing the axis to its true value increases mean FG-PSNR from 15.97 to 29.45dB, with every tested object improving. Axis estimation can therefore limit reconstruction even under regular capture.
